# Growing a love of reading

**The goal is a child who *loves* to read, not just one who *can*.** It's possible to teach a child the
mechanics of reading and simultaneously teach them to dislike it — through drills, pressure, testing,
and joyless practice. The far more valuable aim is a child who reads for pleasure and meaning, because
a child who loves reading will read widely, and reading widely is one of the most powerful engines of
language, knowledge, and thinking there is. Fostering the love matters as much as teaching the skill.

**Reading grows best out of meaning and interest, not drilling.** Children are drawn to reading when it
connects to things they care about and when print clearly *means* something — a story they love, a sign
that tells them something, a joke, a game, a note from a parent. When reading is embedded in interest
and real purpose, children are motivated to crack the code because they want what's on the other side.
When it's reduced to isolated, meaningless drills disconnected from anything the child cares about, it
becomes a chore to be endured and often resisted. Skills still need to be learned and supported, but
they take root far better in the soil of meaning and interest.

**Pressure and fear are especially damaging with reading.** Because reading is so emphasised and so
tied to school success, it's a prime area for anxiety — and, as with all learning, fear undermines it. A
child pushed, tested, compared, and made to feel they're behind or failing at reading can come to
associate books with stress and inadequacy, and avoid them. Children learn to read at genuinely
different ages, all within normal range; pressuring a not-yet-ready child, or one who's struggling,
tends to make things worse by adding anxiety to difficulty. Patience, warmth, and keeping it
pressure-free protect the child's relationship with reading.

**The most powerful thing: read aloud, lots, for joy.** Reading *to* children — long past the age they
can read themselves — is one of the highest-value things a parent can do. It surrounds them with rich
language and story, builds vocabulary and comprehension, and, crucially, ties books to warmth,
closeness, and pleasure. A child who associates reading with cuddling up and enjoying a story with
someone they love is a child being drawn toward reading. Shared, joyful reading does more to create a
reader than any drill.

**How to grow a reader:**
- **Read aloud daily, well past when they can read alone** — for connection and pleasure, not as a
  lesson.
- **Fill life with books and print** — accessible, varied, and matched to the child's interests; let
  them choose.
- **Follow their interests** — comics, facts about sharks, silly stories, whatever hooks them; interest
  beats "quality" in building the habit.
- **Keep it pressure-free and joyful** — no drilling, testing, comparing, or forcing; protect reading
  as a pleasure, not a chore.
- **Model reading** — let children see you reading and enjoying it; readers tend to raise readers.
- **Be patient with pace and struggle** — support without anxiety; get help for genuine, persistent
  difficulty, but don't manufacture pressure over normal variation.
- **Make print meaningful** — notes, lists, signs, recipes, jokes — so reading obviously *does*
  something.

**The one-line version.** Aim for a child who loves reading, not just one who can — grow it by reading
aloud daily for joy, filling life with books matched to their interests, keeping it warm and
pressure-free, modelling reading yourself, and letting reading grow out of meaning and interest rather
than joyless drilling, because a child who loves books will read widely, and that drives everything.
