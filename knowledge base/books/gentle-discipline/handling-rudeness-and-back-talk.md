# Handling rudeness, back-talk, and "attitude"

**What's really happening when your child gets rude.** Somewhere around six and building
through the older years, children start answering back, rolling their eyes, muttering "this
is stupid," or delivering a stinging "you're so unfair." It lands like disrespect, and it's
genuinely hard not to take personally. But back-talk is usually one of three things wearing
a rude costume: a big feeling with poor packaging, a brand-new push for independence and
their own opinion, or a copy of language they've absorbed from siblings, screens, peers —
or, honestly, from us on a bad day. A child testing out "no, and here's why" is doing
developmentally important work; they've just got the tone badly wrong.

**Why the instinctive response makes it worse.** Meeting rudeness with rudeness ("don't you
DARE speak to me like that") answers disrespect with disrespect and models exactly the
thing you're trying to stop. Punishing the *feeling* underneath ("go to your room for being
cheeky") teaches a child to hide feelings rather than handle them. And getting into a
power struggle over tone hands your child enormous power — they've discovered the button
that reliably makes you lose your cool.

**The two-layer response: hold the standard, honour the feeling.** You can do both at once,
and doing both is the whole trick:
1. **Name the standard, calmly and briefly.** "I want to hear you, but not like that."
   "You can be as angry as you like — you can't speak to me meanly." You're not accepting
   the delivery, and you're not escalating either.
2. **Reach for the feeling underneath.** "Something's really bothering you. Tell me the
   angry part — I'm listening." This says the *feeling* is welcome even though the *delivery*
   isn't, and it usually drains the heat far faster than a crackdown.

**Teach the redo — don't just forbid.** Children can't produce a skill they've never been
shown. When things are calm, coach the alternative: "When you're frustrated with me, you
can say 'Mum, I really don't agree' or 'that feels unfair to me.'" Then, in the moment,
offer the do-over warmly: "Try that again in a way I can listen to." A redo teaches the
skill without shame, and children usually take it if you're not being harsh.

**Model the tone you want to hear.** This is the biggest lever and the least comfortable
one. Children speak to us the way we speak to them and to each other. If they hear
sarcasm, contempt, or "because I SAID so," that's the register they'll reach for. Speaking
to your child with the same basic courtesy you'd give a friend — even while holding a firm
line — teaches respect more powerfully than any lecture. And when you slip (everyone does),
repairing it out loud ("I was sharp with you just now, that wasn't kind, I'm sorry")
teaches that respect and repair go together.

**Don't over-read every eye-roll.** Some low-grade grumbling, muttering, and eye-rolling is
a child letting off steam while still doing what was asked — and that's arguably a *win*.
If your child rolls their eyes but gets their shoes on, you can often let the eye-roll go.
Picking a fight over every flicker of attitude turns a small thing into a daily battle and
teaches them that their feelings about your requests aren't allowed. Save your energy for
genuine cruelty, not for evidence that they didn't enjoy being told what to do.

**When rudeness is a pattern, look wider.** A sudden rise in rudeness across the board
often points to something else: a child who's exhausted, worried, struggling at school,
short on connection with you, or steeped in a peer or media culture where that tone is
normal. Persistent contempt is worth a gentle, curious conversation at a calm moment ("I've
noticed things have felt sharp between us lately — how are you doing, really?") rather than
tightening the screws.

**Scripts you can borrow:**
- Instead of "Don't be so rude!" → "That came out mean. Say it again so I can really hear
  you."
- Instead of "How dare you talk back." → "You're allowed to disagree. Tell me why — kindly."
- Instead of matching the sarcasm → a calm "I'll wait for the kind version," then genuinely
  wait.
- Repairing your own slip → "I snapped at you. I'm sorry. Let's start over."

**The one-line version.** Back-talk is usually a big feeling or a bid for independence in a
rude wrapper — hold the standard calmly ("not like that"), welcome the feeling underneath,
teach and invite a kinder redo, model the tone you want, and let the small stuff go.
