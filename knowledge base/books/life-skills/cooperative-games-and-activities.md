# Cooperative games and activities that build skills

**Games are one of the most enjoyable ways children learn life skills.** Structured games and playful
activities — especially *cooperative* ones, where children work together toward a shared goal rather than
competing — are a wonderful vehicle for building social, emotional, and thinking skills. Because they're
fun, children engage willingly and practise skills (turn-taking, teamwork, communication, handling
winning and losing, self-control) without it ever feeling like a lesson. For parents, a repertoire of
simple games is a practical tool for connection *and* development.

**Why cooperative games are especially valuable.** In cooperative games, children succeed or fail
*together*, which teaches teamwork, communication, and mutual support, and removes the sting of one child
losing to another. They're brilliant for:
- **Teamwork and cooperation** — working toward a common goal, coordinating, helping each other.
- **Communication and listening** — many require talking, listening, and working out a plan together.
- **Empathy and inclusion** — everyone's on the same side; no one is left out as the "loser."
- **Problem-solving** — figuring out together how to achieve the goal.
- **Belonging** — the shared success feels good to everyone.
They're especially useful for children who find competition stressful, or for building connection between
siblings or in a group.

**Games that build specific skills (types to reach for):**
- **Turn-taking and patience** — simple board and card games teach waiting, following rules, and coping
  with not going first.
- **Self-control and focus** — "freeze" games, "Simon Says," and "red light/green light" make practising
  impulse control genuinely fun (stop-and-go games are self-regulation training in disguise).
- **Emotion skills** — feelings charades, "name that feeling," and games about reading expressions build
  emotional literacy and empathy.
- **Cooperation** — building something together, team challenges, "keep the balloon up together,"
  parachute games, or any "us vs. the problem" activity.
- **Communication** — describing-and-guessing games, giving-directions games, and games that require
  clear talking and careful listening.
- **Winning and losing gracefully** — any game, used as a chance to coach handling disappointment and
  being a good sport (model it yourself when *you* lose).

**How to get the most out of games:**
- **Play together** — joining in is great for connection and lets you model skills (taking turns,
  losing gracefully, encouraging others).
- **Keep it fun and low-pressure** — the moment it becomes a stressful test, the learning drains out.
  Fun is what keeps children engaged and practising.
- **Coach gently in the moment** — "it's hard to wait, isn't it?", "nice teamwork!", "good losing —
  that was tricky" — naming and encouraging the skill as it happens.
- **Match the game to the child's age and stage** — challenging enough to stretch, easy enough to
  succeed.
- **Use games to practise a struggling skill** — a child who finds turn-taking or losing hard can build
  it through repeated, playful practice far better than through being told off.
- **Debrief lightly afterwards sometimes** — "what helped us win together?" — to draw out the lesson,
  without turning it into a lecture.

**Games also build connection — which underlies everything.** Beyond the specific skills, playing games
*with* your child is simply wonderful for your relationship: shared fun, laughter, and time together fill
the connection tank that makes everything else — cooperation, learning, emotional security — work better.
The bonding is at least as valuable as the skills.

**The one-line version.** Cooperative games and playful activities are an enjoyable, low-pressure way to
build teamwork, communication, self-control, empathy, patience, and good sportsmanship — so keep a
repertoire of simple games, play together, keep it fun, coach the skills gently in the moment, and use
games to practise whatever a child finds hard, all while strengthening your connection.
