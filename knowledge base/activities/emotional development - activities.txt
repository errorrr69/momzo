1. Nurturing Gratitude & Positivity
The Family Gratitude Jar: Each day at bedtime, have everyone write down or talk about one "small victory" or something they are grateful for. Collect these in a jar to review during stressful times
.
"What Went Right" Conversations: Instead of asking "How was your day?", ask "What is one thing that went right today?" focusing on small, positive moments to promote a positive outlook
.
Active Service Projects: Engage your child in community service tailored to their interests, such as collecting items for a food bank or making cards for a nursing home, to build a sense of purpose and contribution
.
2. Building Healthy Self-Esteem
Special One-on-One Time: Dedicate 10–15 minutes, 2–3 times a week, to child-led play. Name it after them (e.g., "Maya’s Time") and follow their lead without asking questions or giving directions
.
The Power of Choice: Provide age-appropriate choices (e.g., "Do you want the blue cup or the red cup?") to help children feel a sense of control and personal power
.
Accepting Mistakes through Modeling: When you make a mistake (like spilling milk), narrate your reaction: "Oops, I made a mistake! It’s okay, I can just wipe it up." This teaches children that setbacks are a normal part of learning
.
3. Calm Communication & Active Listening
Reflective "Mirror" Listening: When your child speaks, summarize what you heard: "It sounds like you’re feeling sad because your tower fell over." This validates their feelings and ensures they feel seen and understood
.
The "I" Statement Game: Practice framing needs positively. Instead of saying "You’re being too loud," model saying "I need a little more quiet while I’m on this call"
.
Get on Their Level: When communicating, physically sit or kneel so you are at or below your child's eye level. This non-verbal cue reduces stress and makes the interaction feel safer for them
.
4. Managing Stress & "Lid Flipping"
The "Hand Brain" Model: Use your hand to teach your child how their brain works. The thumb is the "emotional brain" and the fingers are the "thinking brain." Explain that when we are very upset, we "flip our lid," and the thinking brain goes offline
.
5-4-3-2-1 Grounding: To calm a stressed child, ask them to name 5 things they see, 4 things they hear, 3 things they can touch, 2 things they smell, and 1 thing they taste
.
Parallel Activities: If a child is too stressed for eye contact, engage in "parallel" tasks like coloring together, walking side-by-side, or washing dishes. These activities often make it easier for children to open up
.
5. Emotion Coaching & Regulation
"Sportscasting" (Play-by-Play): Narrate what you see your child doing in a neutral, delighting tone: "You’re using the green blocks to build a very tall house!" This shows them you are present and attentive
.
Feeling Scavenger Hunt: While reading a book together, ask your child to find characters who look "sad," "mad," or "brave." This builds their emotional vocabulary and empathy
.
The Balloon Breath: Teach "belly breathing" by having your child imagine their stomach is a balloon. Have them put a stuffed animal on their belly and watch it "ride the waves" up and down as they breathe deeply
.
6. Shaping Behavior with Positive Attention
The Attention Meter: Give "50 to 100 brief loving touches" a day (a pat on the back, a hug, a hand on the shoulder). This fills their "attention meter" with positive connection so they don't feel the need to act out for attention
.
Catch Them Being Good: Specifically praise effort rather than just results. Say, "I love how hard you worked on sharing that toy" or "Good job using your inside voice"
.
Planned Ignoring: For minor, safe "problem" behaviors used to get attention (like whining), intentionally look away and stop talking. As soon as the behavior stops, immediately re-engage with positive attention: "You’re quiet now; I’m ready to play!"
.
7. Cultivating Kindness & Empathy
The "Caring and Courage Zone": Encourage your child to perform simple acts of kindness, like smiling at a new student or comforting a friend who is sad. Discuss what would make them feel better if they were in that position
.
Modeling Manners: Point out when adults share or use manners. For example: "Daddy is sharing his snack with Mommy. Great job sharing, Daddy!" This shows children that manners aren't just for kids
.
8. Supporting Unique Temperaments
The Gradual Intro (for "Slow-to-Warm" Kids): For children who are shy or hesitant, use "multiple brief exposures." Introduce new people or places gradually and talk about the change beforehand to help them adapt at their own pace
.
Energy Burn (for High-Activity Kids): Provide "safe spaces" for high-energy children to explore and specific times in the day to "burn off" energy through vigorous physical play
.
Clear Cues (for Distractible Kids): When giving instructions, use their name, maintain eye contact, and give only one short, simple direction at a time
.
9. Handling Fear & Anxiety
The "Courage Reward": Create a system to reward "courageous behaviors," like sleeping in their own bed or trying a new activity. Focusing on bravery helps break the cycle of avoidance
.
Disengage from the Tantrum: If a child is using a tantrum to avoid a task (like going to school), maintain a neutral, calm reaction. Avoid "screaming matches," which reinforce the behavior. Once they calm down, immediately engage them in a positive task
.
