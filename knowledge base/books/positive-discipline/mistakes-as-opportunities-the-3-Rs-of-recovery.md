# Mistakes as opportunities — and the 3 R's of recovery

**Reframe the whole idea of getting it wrong.** One of the most freeing ideas in Positive
Discipline is that **mistakes are wonderful opportunities to learn** — not failures to be
punished, hidden, or ashamed of. How a family treats mistakes shapes whether children grow up
willing to try hard things or terrified of failing. If a mistake reliably brings shame or
punishment, children learn to fear, hide, and lie about them. If a mistake brings curiosity and
a chance to put things right, children learn to own them, learn from them, and bounce back —
which is resilience itself.

**This applies to you, the parent, most of all.** You will lose your temper, misjudge, and
handle moments badly — constantly. Positive Discipline is emphatic that *parents'* mistakes are
also opportunities, and that modelling how to recover from them is one of the most valuable
things you can teach. A child who watches a parent make a mistake, own it, and repair it learns
a life skill no lecture could deliver: that being wrong is survivable and mendable, and that
strong people take responsibility.

**The 3 R's of Recovery from a mistake:**
1. **Recognise** — own it without excuses or self-attack. "I made a mistake. I lost my temper
   and shouted." Plain acknowledgement, not "I'm a terrible parent" (that's shame, which helps
   no one) and not "I'm sorry *but* you provoked me" (that's blame in disguise).
2. **Reconcile** — apologise and repair the relationship. "I'm sorry. That wasn't fair to you,
   and it's not how I want to treat you." Children are almost always generous with forgiveness
   when an apology is genuine — and the apology itself reconnects you.
3. **Resolve** — solve the problem together and plan for next time. "Let's figure out a better
   way to handle it when we're both angry." This turns the mistake into shared learning and a
   concrete plan, rather than just a wound that closes over.

**Teach children to use the same three steps.** When your child makes a mistake — breaks
something, hurts a sibling, messes up — resist the urge to punish or force a hollow "say sorry."
Instead, once everyone's calm, walk them through recovery: help them *recognise* what happened
without shaming them, *reconcile* with whoever was affected (a real apology, checking they're
okay), and *resolve* it (help fix or replace what they broke, make a plan for next time). This
teaches responsibility and repair as skills — infinitely more valuable than the fear a
punishment installs.

**Separate the deed from the child — and never pile on.** "You made a mistake" must never
become "you *are* a mistake." Keep the child's worth entirely intact while addressing the
behaviour: "You're a good kid who did something that didn't work out — let's put it right." And
skip the "I told you so" and the lecture; a child buried in shame can't do the useful work of
recovering, and shame reliably produces *more* misbehaviour, not less.

**Why this matters so much for confidence.** A child who believes mistakes are catastrophes
plays small, avoids challenges, gives up easily, and hides the truth. A child who believes
mistakes are how everyone learns will attempt hard things, persist through failure, tell you
when something's gone wrong, and recover with dignity. Which of those you raise depends largely
on the emotional weather you create around getting things wrong — day after ordinary day.

**Make "what did you learn?" a family habit.** Over dinner, share a mistake from your own day
and what it taught you; invite the same from your children, lightly and without judgement. It
normalises imperfection, models growth, and makes your home a place where it's safe to be a
learner — which is exactly what a child is.

**The one-line version.** Treat every mistake — yours and your child's — as a chance to learn,
not a crime to punish: Recognise it honestly, Reconcile through genuine apology and repair, and
Resolve it together for next time, keeping the child's worth fully intact so they grow up
resilient and honest rather than ashamed and hiding.
