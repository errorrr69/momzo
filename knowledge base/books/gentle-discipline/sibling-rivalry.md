# Sibling rivalry — fighting, jealousy, and "it's not fair"

**What's really going on.** Sibling conflict is not a sign you've done something wrong; it's
one of the most normal things in family life. Brothers and sisters are competing for the
most precious resource in a child's world — *your* love and attention — while also learning,
in the safest possible arena, how to share, negotiate, lose, and make up. Underneath most
squabbles is a simple worry: "is there enough of you for me?" A child who feels secure in
their own bond with you has far less need to fight for it.

**Why "who started it?" is a trap.** Playing judge — deciding who's guilty, who gets
punished, who was right — pulls you into every fight, teaches children to run to you as
referee rather than sort things out, and quietly casts one child as the "bad" one. It also
usually fails, because by the time you arrive you've missed the real start. Stepping out of
the judge's chair is one of the most freeing shifts a parent can make.

**Your role: coach, not referee.** 
- **When it's low-level bickering, stay out.** Learning to resolve conflict *is* the skill;
  they can't practise it if you settle everything. Narrate faith in them: "You two are having
  a hard time sharing that. I trust you can find a fair way — call me if you get stuck."
- **When it escalates to hurting, step in for safety, not blame.** Stop the harm calmly,
  name both sides' feelings, and hand the problem back: "You both want the tablet. Hitting
  isn't okay. So — how can two people share one tablet?" You facilitate; they solve.
- **Describe, don't accuse.** "I see two kids who both want the same spot and nobody's
  budging," rather than "why are you always so mean to her?"

**Treat "fair" as *individual*, not *identical*.** Children have a radar for fairness, but
trying to make everything exactly equal is an unwinnable trap that actually feeds rivalry
(they'll compare every scoop of ice cream forever). Aim instead for each child getting what
*they* need. A helpful line: "I love you both completely, and I give each of you what you
need — that won't always look the same." When one protests "that's not fair!", you can hear
the feeling ("you wish you had one too") without promising sameness.

**Protect each child's separateness.** Rivalry grows in comparison. Avoid labels and
match-ups ("why can't you be tidy like your sister?", "he's the sporty one"), even
flattering ones — a role assigned to one child is a role denied to the other, and children
live up (and down) to the stories we tell about them. Notice and enjoy each child for their
own qualities, out of the other's earshot when it matters.

**Fill each child's tank one-to-one.** The single most powerful anti-rivalry move isn't a
technique in the heat of a fight — it's regular, unhurried, one-on-one time with each child
where they have you completely to themselves, even ten or fifteen minutes. A child topped up
with your attention has far less reason to knock their sibling to get it. A lot of
"jealousy" is simply a connection tank running low.

**Help them see each other as allies, not rivals.** Point out kindnesses ("your brother
saved you a seat"), give them shared missions where they're on the same team rather than in
competition, and resist setting them against each other ("first one dressed wins!"), which
teaches that a sibling's loss is your gain. The long game is a friendship for life, and
that's built by casting them as teammates.

**Handle the big feelings behind the fights.** Jealousy, especially, is a painful,
shameful feeling children can't help. Let it be sayable without punishment: "It's okay to
feel jealous — most people do sometimes. You can tell me, and I'll still love you exactly
the same." A feeling that's allowed and understood loses much of its power to drive
behaviour.

**Scripts you can borrow:**
- "You're both really cross about this. I know you can sort out something fair — I'll check
  back in two minutes."
- "I won't let anyone get hurt. Tell me the problem, one at a time."
- "You wish you had what she has. That makes sense. You'll get your turn with me too."
- "You don't have to like your brother every minute. You do have to keep him safe."

**The one-line version.** Sibling rivalry is children competing for your love and practising
conflict in a safe place — step out of the judge's chair, coach fair solutions instead of
settling them, keep each child's tank full with one-on-one time, treat fairness as
individual not identical, and cast them as teammates, not rivals.
