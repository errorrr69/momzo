# Understanding focus, attention, and the very active child

**Start with the reframe: attention is a developing skill, not a measure of willpower.** When
a 6–10 year old can't sit still, forgets what you just said, drifts off mid-task, or bounces
from one thing to the next, it's easy to read it as laziness, defiance, or "not trying." Almost
always it's neither. The brain systems that run attention, sitting still, and self-control are
still under construction at this age, and they develop at very different rates in different
children. A child who struggles to focus is usually working *harder* than their peers to do the
same task, not slacking off.

**Why some children find focus much harder.** Children vary enormously in temperament and brain
wiring. Some are naturally more active, more distractible, more driven by what's interesting
right now, and slower to develop the internal "brakes." For these children — whether or not
there's ever a formal label like ADHD — the same demands (sit through a lesson, do homework,
wait your turn, remember three instructions) cost far more effort. This is a difference in how
their brain is built, not a flaw in their character, and treating it as misbehaviour to be
punished makes everything worse.

**The interest paradox — "but he can focus for hours on Lego!"** Parents often notice that a
child who "can't concentrate" will focus intensely on a game, a build, or a video for ages, and
conclude the focus problem must be willful. In fact this is classic: attention systems that
struggle with effortful, less-interesting tasks often work *fine*, even too well, on things that
are novel, fast-moving, or deeply engaging. The child isn't choosing to focus only on the fun
stuff; their attention is driven by interest and stimulation more than most. Understanding this
stops you taking the "he could if he wanted to" view, which isn't accurate or helpful.

**Movement and fidgeting often *help*, not hinder.** For active, distractible children,
wriggling, doodling, standing up, or having something to fidget with can actually improve
focus, not wreck it — the movement keeps their brain at the right level of alertness to attend.
Forcing rigid stillness ("sit STILL and concentrate!") can make concentration harder. Allowing
some movement (a wobble cushion, a stress ball, standing to work, a lap around the room between
tasks) works with the brain instead of against it.

**These children are not "difficult" — they have real strengths.** A framing that protects your
child's spirit: high-energy, distractible kids are frequently creative, enthusiastic,
spontaneous, curious, funny, kind-hearted, and able to hyperfocus on passions. The very traits
that make school hard (energy, novelty-seeking, thinking sideways) can become gifts. Children
absorb the story we tell about them — "you're so lazy/naughty/all over the place" becomes a
self-image, while "you've got a busy, curious brain and we'll find ways that work for it" builds
a child who believes in themselves.

**What this understanding changes in how you parent:**
- **Swap "won't" for "can't yet."** Assume a skill gap, not defiance, and ask "what support
  would help?" rather than "what punishment will teach him?"
- **Adjust the environment, not just the child.** Much of the help is external — structure,
  reminders, movement, chunking tasks — rather than expecting the child to summon focus they
  don't yet have.
- **Protect the relationship and self-esteem.** These children get corrected, told off, and
  compared *constantly*, at home and school. They need extra doses of noticing what they do
  well, or they quietly conclude they're "the bad kid."
- **Partner with school and, when needed, professionals.** If focus and activity levels are
  significantly and persistently affecting learning, friendships, or happiness across settings,
  that's worth a calm conversation with the school and your child's doctor — not as a failure,
  but to get the right support.

**The one-line version.** Trouble focusing and sitting still is a still-developing, differently
wired brain working hard — not laziness or defiance — so read it as "can't yet," allow movement,
build in external structure, protect your child's self-image, and celebrate the real strengths
that come with a busy, curious mind.
