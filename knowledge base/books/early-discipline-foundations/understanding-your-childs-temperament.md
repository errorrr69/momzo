# Understanding your child's temperament

**Temperament is the "nature" your child was born with.** From very early on, children come
wired with a characteristic style — how intensely they feel things, how they react to newness,
how active they are, how easily they adapt, how sensitive they are to noise, textures, and
change. This is temperament, and it's largely innate: it's not something you caused by
parenting, and it's not something a child chooses. It's the raw material you're working with,
and it lasts — the intense, sensitive toddler is usually recognisable in the intense, sensitive
nine-year-old. Understanding your particular child's nature is one of the most useful things a
parent can do, because it turns "why is my child *like this*?" into "this is who my child is —
how do I work *with* it?"

**Some of the traits that vary child to child:**
- **Intensity** — how big their reactions are. Some children feel and express everything at full
  volume (huge joy, huge distress); others are mild and even.
- **Sensitivity** — how strongly they register sights, sounds, textures, tastes, and emotional
  atmosphere. Highly sensitive children notice everything and are easily overwhelmed by too much.
- **Adaptability** — how easily they handle change and transitions. Some flow; others need
  warning and time.
- **Approach vs withdrawal** — do they dive into new situations, or hang back and warm up slowly?
- **Activity level** — how much they need to move.
- **Persistence** — do they stick doggedly at things (and dig in stubbornly), or give up easily?
- **Regularity** — how predictable their sleep, hunger, and moods are.
- **Mood** — their general sunny-to-serious baseline.

**"Goodness of fit" — the key idea.** No temperament is good or bad; what matters is the *fit*
between a child's nature, their environment, and their parents' expectations and style. A
high-energy child in a household that expects long stillness will clash; the same child with room
to move thrives. A slow-to-warm child pushed to "just say hello!" freezes; given time, they
settle. A huge amount of family friction is really a *mismatch* between a child's wiring and what's
being asked of them — and the fix is usually to adjust the expectations and environment to fit the
child, not to try to change the child's fundamental nature.

**Work with the grain, not against it.** You can't sand a spirited child into a placid one, or
toughen a sensitive child by exposure — and trying tends to make a child feel there's something
wrong with them. Instead:
- **Anticipate and accommodate.** Know your child's triggers (the sensitive child needs downtime
  after a busy day; the slow-to-adapt child needs transition warnings; the intense child needs
  help with big feelings) and build life around them where you reasonably can.
- **Name it kindly, never as a flaw.** "You feel things really deeply" beats "you're so
  dramatic"; "you like to watch before you join in" beats "why are you so shy?" Children live up
  to the labels we give them.
- **Find the strength in every trait.** Intensity → passion and enthusiasm. Sensitivity →
  empathy and depth. Persistence → determination. Cautiousness → thoughtfulness. High energy →
  drive. The very traits that challenge you often become your child's greatest assets, if they're
  not shamed out of them.

**Match your parenting to *this* child.** Siblings with different temperaments need genuinely
different handling, and what worked for one may fail for another — that's not inconsistency,
it's good fit. The sensitive child needs extra gentleness and calm; the spirited child needs
outlets and firm-but-warm structure; the cautious child needs patience and no pushing. Reading
your individual child and adjusting to them is the heart of responsive parenting.

**The one-line version.** Your child is born with a temperament — their intensity, sensitivity,
adaptability, energy, and more — that you didn't cause and can't fundamentally change; the goal
is a good "fit," so learn your particular child's nature, adjust expectations and environment to
work with the grain, name each trait as a strength rather than a flaw, and parent *this* child
rather than a child you expected.
