# Whining, sulking, and big emotions

**Why whining grates — and what it actually is.** Whining is one of the most nerve-shredding
sounds a parent knows, and that's almost by design: it's a distress signal built to be hard
to ignore. A child whines when they want something and feel powerless to get it, or when
they're running low — tired, hungry, over it — and haven't got the composure to ask
properly. It isn't a plot to annoy you; it's a depleted child using a depleted child's
voice. Sulking is the older, quieter cousin: a child flooded with disappointment or hurt
who has gone inward because they don't yet know how to say what they feel.

**The trap: whining works, so it grows.** If whining sometimes gets the biscuit, the extra
screen time, or even just a big reaction from you, the behaviour is being fed — intermittently,
which is exactly the pattern that makes something stubborn. The fix isn't to punish the
whine; it's to stop letting the *whining tone* be the thing that works, while still warmly
meeting the *need* underneath.

**Respond to the need, retrain the delivery.** Two moves, together:
1. **Don't reward the whine, don't punish the child.** Calmly: "I can't understand that
   voice — tell me in your strong/normal voice and I'll really listen." Say it kindly, not
   as a gotcha. Then genuinely listen the moment they switch.
2. **Name and meet the feeling.** Often the whine is powerlessness. "You really wanted to
   keep playing and it's time to stop — that's so disappointing." Being understood does more
   to end a whine than any amount of "stop whining."

**Naming feelings is a skill you teach, and it's the master key.** A huge amount of "bad
behaviour" at 6–10 is simply big emotion with no words and no route. Children who can *say*
"I'm frustrated / left out / nervous / gutted" have far less need to whine, sulk, or lash
out. You build this by narrating feelings out loud — theirs ("you look really let down"),
yours ("I'm feeling frazzled, I need a minute"), and characters' in books and shows. Over
time the child internalises a vocabulary for the storm, and words are the beginning of
control.

**Let feelings be feelings — don't rush to fix or dismiss.** Our instinct is to make the
bad feeling go away fast: "don't cry, it's fine," "there's no reason to be upset," "you're
okay!" However kindly meant, this teaches a child that their feelings are wrong or too much.
Big emotions aren't a problem to be solved; they're weather to be weathered *with* your
child. Sitting alongside — "this is hard, I'm right here" — and letting the feeling run its
course tells them feelings are safe, survivable, and don't cost them your love. That is how
emotional regulation is actually built: not by shutting feelings down, but by riding them
out in the safety of your presence.

**Sulking needs patience, not pursuit or punishment.** A sulking child is often too flooded
to talk and needs a bridge back, not a demand. Chasing them ("don't you sulk at me!") or
punishing the mood pushes them further in. Instead: acknowledge and leave the door open —
"You're really upset. I'll be right here when you're ready, no rush." A gentle, low-pressure
offer (a snuggle, a shared task, a bit of humour once the edge is off) usually lets them
come back with dignity intact. The goal isn't to force them to cheer up; it's to make
coming back easy.

**Prevention: mind the tank and the timing.** Whining and sulking spike with the same
predictable culprits as everything else — tiredness, hunger, over-stimulation, transitions,
and a low connection tank. A snack, a slower transition, ten minutes of your full attention
before you need them to cooperate, and enough sleep will prevent more meltdowns than any
in-the-moment technique. Front-loading connection ("filling the tank") is especially
powerful: a child who feels close to you has far less need to whine for the crumbs of your
attention.

**What to avoid:**
- **Mocking or mimicking the whine.** It shames without teaching and damages trust.
- **"You're fine / stop crying / there's nothing to be upset about."** It denies a real
  feeling and teaches concealment.
- **Giving in to end the noise.** Understandable, but it trains the whine. Meet the *need*
  warmly; don't let the *tone* be what unlocks the yes.

**Scripts you can borrow:**
- "I can't hear that whiny voice well — say it in your big-kid voice and I'm all ears."
- "You wanted that so much. It's a no today, and I know that's really disappointing."
- "You seem flat and grumpy. Something's up. I'm here whenever you want to tell me."
- "It's okay to cry. I'll stay with you until it feels a bit better."

**The one-line version.** Whining and sulking are depleted, powerless children without the
words for a big feeling — don't reward the tone or punish the mood, name and meet the need,
teach feeling-words, let emotions run their course in your calm company, and prevent the
predictable dips.
