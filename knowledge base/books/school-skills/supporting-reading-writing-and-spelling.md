# Supporting reading, writing, and spelling

**Your role is to support and encourage, not to run a second school.** Parents don't need to be teachers
to help their child with literacy — and turning home into a pressured classroom usually backfires. The
most powerful things you can do are keep literacy warm, meaningful, and low-pressure, read together
daily, and support the specific skills gently. A child who associates reading and writing with pleasure
and success will thrive far more than one drilled into resentment.

**Reading rests on two things together: decoding and meaning.** Learning to read involves both cracking
the code (letters make sounds, sounds make words — phonics) and understanding what it means. Both matter:
- **Phonics / decoding** — children learn to map letters to sounds and blend them. Playing with sounds
  (rhymes, "what sound does it start with?", clapping syllables) and gentle practice support this.
- **Meaning and love of books** — reading is *for* something; keep it tied to interest and enjoyment
  (see the note on growing a love of reading). Reading aloud daily, well past when they can read alone,
  builds vocabulary, comprehension, and motivation.
Keep practice little, often, warm, and pressure-free; let the child read things they're interested in;
and celebrate progress, not perfection.

**Writing and handwriting develop with support and practice.** Writing is demanding — it combines fine
motor control, spelling, and organising thoughts. Help by:
- **Supporting the physical side** — a comfortable pencil grip, good posture, and plenty of fine-motor
  play (drawing, cutting, building, threading) that strengthens the hands.
- **Separating "getting ideas down" from "getting it perfect"** — let first drafts be messy; ideas
  first, neatness and spelling later, so children aren't paralysed.
- **Giving real reasons to write** — cards, lists, notes, stories, messages — so writing obviously
  *does* something.
- **Keeping it encouraging** — praise effort and content, don't drown their writing in red-pen
  corrections.

**Spelling: patterns, multisensory practice, little and often.** Spelling is learned best not by rote
misery but through:
- **Understanding patterns and rules** — English spelling has logic (common patterns, letter strings,
  word families); noticing patterns beats memorising random lists.
- **Multisensory practice** — see it, say it, hear it, write it, even trace it; engaging several senses
  helps it stick (a classic sequence: look, say, cover, write, check).
- **Little and often** — short, frequent practice beats long, stressful sessions.
- **Learning from their own errors** — noticing and correcting the specific words a child gets wrong,
  kindly, is more useful than generic lists.
- **Low pressure** — anxiety impairs memory; keep it calm and even playful (word games, magnetic
  letters).

**Every child learns at their own pace — and some need extra help.** Children reach reading, writing,
and spelling milestones at genuinely different ages, all normal. Comparison and pressure over normal
variation do harm. But some children have specific difficulties — persistent, significant struggles with
reading or spelling despite effort and support can be a sign of dyslexia or a related difficulty. Signs
worth noting include ongoing trouble linking letters and sounds, very effortful reading, and spelling
that stays highly irregular. This is not about intelligence or effort, and identifying it early and
getting the right support (from school or a specialist) makes a big difference. Trust your instincts and
ask for help if a child is persistently struggling.

**The through-line: keep it warm, meaningful, and pressure-free.** Across reading, writing, and spelling,
the same principles apply: tie skills to meaning and interest, practise little and often, praise effort
and progress, keep anxiety out, read and write together for real purposes, and be patient with pace.
Protecting a child's confidence and enjoyment matters as much as the skills themselves — because a
confident, willing learner keeps going, and a discouraged one shuts down.

**The one-line version.** Support literacy by keeping it warm, meaningful, and low-pressure — read aloud
daily, back both phonics and love of books, separate getting-ideas-down from getting-it-perfect in
writing, teach spelling through patterns and short multisensory practice, praise effort over perfection,
respect each child's pace, and seek help early for persistent, significant struggles.
