# The sensitive or spirited child

**Some children feel everything more.** A meaningful minority of children are, by nature, highly
sensitive or "spirited" — they experience the world at higher volume than most. They feel
emotions more intensely, notice every sound, smell, texture, and shift in mood, get overwhelmed
by busy or new environments, react strongly to small frustrations, and can be harder to soothe.
This isn't a disorder, a phase, or the result of "soft" parenting — it's a temperament, and it
persists. The sensitive toddler who melted down in the supermarket is often the sensitive
eight-year-old who unravels after a loud, over-scheduled day. Recognising this changes everything
about how you help them.

**Why they behave the way they do.** A sensitive or spirited child's big reactions and frequent
overwhelm come from a nervous system that takes in more and tips over sooner. What looks like
"overreacting," drama, or being difficult is genuinely how intensely they experience things.
Punishing the intensity ("stop being so dramatic," "toughen up") tells a child that their very
nature is unacceptable — which is deeply wounding and doesn't work anyway, because they can't
change how much they feel. They can only learn, slowly and with help, to manage it.

**What these children need more of:**
- **Gentleness and calm.** Harshness, shouting, and big consequences overwhelm a sensitive child
  fast and shut them down. They respond far better to a soft voice, patience, and a calm adult who
  lends them steadiness. Your regulation is doubly important with these kids.
- **Downtime and low stimulation.** They need more quiet, more recovery time, and protection from
  over-scheduling and sensory overload. A slow morning, a calm corner, unstructured downtime after
  school, and fewer back-to-back activities prevent a lot of meltdowns. Build in decompression.
- **Warning and preparation.** Changes and transitions hit them harder, so heads-up, predictable
  routines, and previewing what's coming ("after the party we'll come home and have quiet time")
  help enormously.
- **Help naming and riding big feelings.** They especially need a rich feelings vocabulary and a
  calm companion while the storm passes (not a fixer or a dismisser). "This is a lot right now,
  I'm here" is exactly right.

**Reframe the traits as strengths — and guard their self-image.** Sensitive, spirited children
are frequently deeply empathetic, perceptive, caring, imaginative, passionate, and attuned to
others' feelings — the flip side of feeling everything. But because they're "a lot," they often
get a stream of negative messages ("why are you like this," "you're too much," "calm down") and
can grow up believing they're flawed. Actively protect their sense of self: name their sensitivity
as a gift ("you notice how everyone's feeling — that's a real kindness"), and let them know their
big heart is exactly right, not a problem.

**Manage the environment as much as the child.** A great deal of helping a sensitive child is
prevention through environment: enough sleep, enough downtime, warning before changes, not too
much noise/crowd/screen, and steady routines. Get the conditions right and the "behaviour"
often settles on its own, because you've stopped pushing a sensitive system past its limits.

**Look after yourself, too.** Parenting an intense, hard-to-soothe child is genuinely tiring, and
it's easy to feel you're doing something wrong when nothing seems to calm them. You're not — this
is a demanding temperament, and it asks a lot of you. Your steady, gentle presence is the single
best regulator they have, so keeping your own cup filled (support, breaks, patience with yourself)
is part of helping them.

**The one-line version.** Sensitive and spirited children feel everything at higher volume by
nature, so their big reactions aren't overreacting or bad behaviour — meet them with extra
gentleness, downtime, warning before change, and help riding their feelings, manage the
environment to prevent overload, and protect their self-image by naming their depth of feeling as
the gift it is.
