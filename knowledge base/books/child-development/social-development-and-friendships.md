# Social development and friendships (6–10)

**Peers move to centre stage.** In middle childhood, the social world expands dramatically and
friendships become enormously important. Where younger children mostly orbit their family, 6–10 year
olds increasingly care about, learn from, and define themselves through their peers. Friendships
become a central source of fun, belonging, support, and identity — and also of some of childhood's
sharpest hurts. This shift is healthy and necessary: learning to navigate the peer world is a core
developmental task of this age.

**How the understanding of friendship matures.** Children's very concept of friendship deepens over
these years:
- Early on, a "friend" is largely someone you play with and who's nearby or shares toys — concrete
  and activity-based.
- Gradually it becomes about *trust, loyalty, kindness, and mutual support* — a friend is someone
  who helps you, keeps your secrets, sticks up for you, and whom you can rely on. By the end of this
  stage children value closeness, shared understanding, and being able to count on each other.
This growing depth means friendships become more emotionally significant — and their breakdowns more
painful.

**Key social skills develop — and can be taught.** Successful peer relationships rest on skills that
grow across this age and that children can be coached in:
- **Perspective-taking** — increasingly understanding others' thoughts, feelings, and intentions
  (they become less egocentric), which is the foundation of empathy and getting along.
- **Cooperation and sharing** — working and playing together toward common goals, taking turns,
  compromising.
- **Conflict resolution** — negotiating, handling disagreements, and repairing after fights (a huge
  amount of learning happens through working out squabbles).
- **Reading social cues** — picking up on tone, body language, and group dynamics.
- **Managing emotions socially** — handling frustration, losing a game, or exclusion without falling
  apart.

**Belonging, groups, and social comparison.** Children this age increasingly want to fit in, join
groups, and be accepted, and they constantly *compare* themselves to peers — who's good at what, who
has what, who's in and who's out. This social comparison shapes self-esteem (see the self-concept
note) and can be a source of both motivation and anxiety. Groups, "best friends," and — toward the
older end — cliques become prominent, along with a strong sense of fairness.

**The hard side: rejection, exclusion, and bullying.** With peers mattering so much, social pain
lands hard. Being left out, falling out with a best friend, or being teased or bullied can deeply
affect a child at this age. Some children are more socially skilled or accepted than others, and
those who struggle socially (through shyness, aggression, difference, or missing skills) may need
extra support. Bullying is a serious issue that thrives on children's intense need to belong;
children need to know they can tell a trusted adult and will be helped, not dismissed.

**Play is where much of this is learned.** Especially social and pretend play, games with rules, and
free play with peers are the training ground for cooperation, negotiation, turn-taking, fairness,
and reading others. Protecting time for real, unstructured play with other children is protecting
social development.

**How parents support social development:**
- **Provide opportunities** — time with peers, playdates, group activities, unstructured play.
- **Coach skills, don't just fix** — help them think through social problems ("what could you try?"),
  role-play tricky situations, and reflect on how others might feel, rather than solving every
  conflict for them.
- **Model good relating** — children learn friendship skills from how you treat others and handle
  your own conflicts.
- **Take social pain seriously** — listen, validate ("that really hurt"), and support without
  rushing to fix or minimising; help them problem-solve and know when to step in (e.g. bullying).
- **Don't over-manage** — let them work out ordinary squabbles; struggling through minor conflicts
  is how the skills grow.
- **Nurture empathy and kindness** — talk about others' feelings, notice and value kind behaviour.
- **Support the child who struggles socially** — some children need explicit help with skills,
  smaller settings, or finding like-minded friends around a shared interest.

**The one-line version.** In middle childhood peers and friendships become central, children's
understanding of friendship deepens toward trust and loyalty, and crucial social skills
(perspective-taking, cooperation, conflict resolution) develop largely through play and real
experience — so give children plenty of peer time, coach rather than fix their social problems, take
exclusion and bullying seriously, and nurture empathy and kindness.
