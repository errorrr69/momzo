# Keeping your own cool — your triggers, and why they matter most

**The hardest truth of gentle discipline: it starts with you, not the child.** You cannot
pour calm into a child from an empty, jangled cup. When a child's behaviour lights you up —
the whining, the defiance, the tenth interruption — your own emotional brain floods just like
theirs, and a flooded adult can't help a flooded child. So the most important discipline
skill isn't a technique you use *on* your child; it's the ability to steady *yourself* first.
This isn't a nice extra. It's the foundation everything else stands on.

**Why your child pushes *your* buttons specifically.** Certain behaviours enrage us far
beyond what they warrant, and it's worth being curious about that. Often the triggers that
flood us most were shaped in our own childhoods — the things we weren't allowed to do, the
feelings that weren't safe in the home we grew up in, the ways we were disciplined. When your
child does the very thing that would have got *you* in trouble, an old, deep alarm can fire.
Recognising "this reaction is bigger than this moment" gives you a precious half-second of
choice.

**Co-regulation: your calm is the intervention.** Children literally borrow their parents'
nervous systems to settle — a steady, warm adult presence physically helps a child's body
come down from distress. The reverse is also true: your panic, anger, or sharpness pours
fuel on their fire. This means that on the hardest days, the single most effective thing you
can do for your child's behaviour is manage your own state. Your regulation *is* the tool.

**In the heat of the moment — steady yourself first:**
- **Notice the surge before you act.** The tight chest, the heat, the urge to shout — that's
  your early-warning system. Catching it early is everything.
- **Pause and breathe.** A few slow breaths, especially a long out-breath, tells your body
  the emergency is over and brings your thinking brain back online. Even three seconds helps.
- **Buy time out loud.** "I'm getting frustrated. I need a moment before I respond." This is
  not weakness — it *models* the exact skill you want your child to learn, and it's far
  better than reacting and regretting.
- **Step back if safe.** If everyone's safe, it's fine to say "I'm going to get a glass of
  water and come back calmer." Leaving to regulate is responsible, not abandoning.
- **Lower and slow your voice instead of raising it.** Going quieter is disarming and helps
  you both.

**Between the storms — fill your own cup.** How you cope in the moment depends hugely on your
baseline. A parent running on no sleep, no support, no breaks, and a full load of stress has
almost no reserve, and will flood at the smallest thing — that's biology, not weakness. Real
self-care here isn't a luxury; it's part of the job:
- Protect sleep and food where you possibly can.
- Ask for and accept help; you were never meant to do this alone.
- Take even small breaks to top up your own reserves.
- Notice and challenge the thoughts that inflate the moment ("he's doing this *at* me," "I'm
  a terrible mother") — they pour petrol on your reactions.

**Let go of perfection — and drop the guilt.** You will lose your temper. Every parent does.
The goal is not a parent who never gets angry (that parent doesn't exist); it's a parent who
ruptures *less*, catches themselves *sooner*, and *repairs* afterwards. Guilt that spirals
into shame actually makes you a shorter-fused parent, not a better one. Aim for "good enough,"
forgive yourself the bad moments, and focus on the repair.

**Repair is the superpower.** When you do snap — and you will — coming back to your child and
owning it ("I shouted, and that wasn't fair to you. I'm sorry. I was overwhelmed, and I'm
going to try to handle it better") does something remarkable. It doesn't undo your authority;
it *models* accountability, teaches your child that big feelings and mistakes are survivable
and mendable, and rebuilds the connection. Children don't need a perfect parent. They need
one who keeps coming back.

**The one-line version.** You can't calm a child while you're flooded yourself, so the core
skill is regulating *you* first — notice your surge, breathe, buy time, know which of your
child's behaviours hook your own history, keep your own cup filled, ditch the guilt, and
repair every time you slip.
