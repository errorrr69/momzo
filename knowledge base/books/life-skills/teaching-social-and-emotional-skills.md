# Teaching social and emotional skills — practical ways

**Social-emotional skills are taught, and everyday moments are the classroom.** Skills like naming
feelings, calming down, showing empathy, making friends, sharing, taking turns, and resolving conflict
don't just appear with age — they're learned, and parents teach them constantly through ordinary
interactions, conversations, play, and modelling. You don't need a curriculum; you need to notice the
teachable moments and know a few simple ways to build each skill. These skills matter enormously — they
predict friendships, wellbeing, and even school success — so treating them as worth teaching is time
well spent.

**Teaching children to name and understand feelings.**
- **Narrate emotions constantly** — theirs ("you look really disappointed"), yours ("I'm feeling
  frazzled"), and characters' in books and shows. A rich feelings vocabulary is the foundation of
  emotional skill; you can't manage what you can't name.
- **Use a feelings chart or "how big is the feeling?" scale** to help children identify and gauge
  emotions.
- **Talk about what feelings feel like in the body** (butterflies, hot face, tight fists) so children
  can catch them early.
- **Accept all feelings** while guiding behaviour — "it's okay to feel angry; it's not okay to hit."

**Teaching calming and self-regulation.**
- **Practise calming tools in calm moments** (not mid-meltdown) so they're available later: slow belly
  breaths (a long out-breath is the fastest off-switch), counting, a "calm corner" with soft things,
  squeezing or pushing something, going somewhere quiet.
- **Make a personal "calm-down plan" together** — let the child choose what helps them.
- **Model it out loud** — "I'm frustrated, I'm going to take three big breaths."

**Teaching empathy and kindness.**
- **Point out others' feelings** — "look how happy she is that you shared," "he seems left out."
- **Ask perspective-taking questions** — "how do you think she felt when that happened?"
- **Notice and value kind acts** specifically — "that was really thoughtful of you to help."
- **Read stories and talk about characters' feelings and choices** — fiction is a superb empathy gym.
- **Model kindness and caring** — children copy how they see you treat people.

**Teaching friendship and social skills.**
- **Coach the specifics** — how to join a game ("can I play?"), take turns, share, listen, disagree
  kindly, and handle losing.
- **Role-play and rehearse** tricky situations in advance (starting a conversation, handling teasing) —
  practice builds confidence.
- **Problem-solve social bumps together** rather than fixing them — "what could you try?" — so children
  build their own skills.
- **Provide lots of real practice** — playdates, group play, unstructured time with peers, where these
  skills actually develop.

**Teaching conflict resolution.**
- **Coach a simple process** — calm down first, each person says how they feel and what they want,
  then brainstorm a fair solution together.
- **Step back and let them try** on low-stakes squabbles (with hitting off-limits), so they practise —
  working out conflicts *is* the learning.
- **Model repair** — apologise and reconcile after your own slips, so children learn how.

**The principles that make it all work.**
- **Teach in calm moments, support in hot ones** — lessons don't land mid-storm.
- **Model everything** — your own emotional handling, kindness, and conflict style teach more than any
  instruction.
- **Practice, practice, practice** — through play, role-play, stories, and real life; skills grow with
  reps.
- **Notice and encourage** the skills when you see them, far more than you correct their absence.
- **Be patient** — these are complex skills built over years; expect lots of stumbles.

**The one-line version.** Social-emotional skills — naming feelings, calming down, empathy, friendship,
conflict resolution — are taught through everyday moments, play, stories, role-play, and above all your
modelling; teach them in calm moments, coach the specifics, give lots of real practice, and notice them
warmly, because these skills shape a child's whole wellbeing and relationships.
