# Emotional development in middle childhood (6–10)

**Emotions get more complex, and children get better at handling them.** Across middle childhood,
children's emotional lives grow richer and more nuanced, and — gradually, unevenly — their ability
to manage feelings matures. A six-year-old and a ten-year-old are worlds apart emotionally: the
older child understands feelings more deeply, has more strategies for coping, and is far more aware
of how emotions work in themselves and others. But this is a *developing* capacity built on a
still-maturing brain, so big feelings and wobbles remain completely normal throughout.

**Understanding emotions deepens.** School-age children develop several key insights:
- **Mixed and conflicting emotions** — they come to understand you can feel two things at once
  (excited *and* nervous about a new school; loving a sibling *and* being furious at them). Younger
  children struggle with this; older ones grasp it, which helps them make sense of their inner life.
- **That feelings can be hidden or masked** — they realise the emotion you *show* can differ from
  what you *feel*, and start managing their emotional expression (putting on a brave face,
  disappointment about a gift).
- **That the same event affects people differently** — a growing understanding that others may feel
  differently than they do about the same thing.
- **The causes and course of emotions** — they get better at understanding why they and others feel
  as they do, which supports empathy and problem-solving.

**Self-conscious emotions come to the fore.** Middle childhood is when emotions like pride, guilt,
shame, and embarrassment become powerful, tied to the child's growing sense of self and their
awareness of others' judgments. Healthy guilt (feeling bad about a specific action) supports
conscience and repair; toxic shame (feeling bad about *who you are*) undermines self-worth. This is
a key reason to address behaviour without shaming the child — "that was unkind" (guilt, fixable)
rather than "you're a bad kid" (shame, corrosive).

**Emotional regulation is the central developing skill.** The heart of emotional development at this
age is learning to *manage* feelings — to calm down when upset, cope with disappointment and
frustration, and not be swept away by every emotion. This grows through:
- **The maturing brain** — the self-control centre is slowly developing (but far from done, so
  expect lapses).
- **Co-regulation** — children learn to self-soothe by being calmed, again and again, by a steady
  caregiver; your calm literally teaches their nervous system how to settle.
- **Practice and teaching** — naming feelings, learning coping strategies, and being coached through
  emotional moments.
Children this age develop more strategies (distracting themselves, reframing a situation, seeking
comfort, talking it out) — but under stress, tiredness, or hunger, regulation still collapses, and
that's normal, not a failure.

**Emotional skills predict a lot.** A child's emotional competence — recognising and managing their
own feelings and reading others' — is strongly linked to friendships, school success, and mental
health, arguably as much as raw intelligence. Helping a child build these skills is not "soft"; it's
foundational to their whole wellbeing and future.

**How parents nurture emotional development:**
- **Name feelings, all of them.** Narrate emotions — theirs, yours, characters' in stories — so
  children build a rich feelings vocabulary. Naming a feeling is the first step to taming it.
- **Accept feelings while guiding behaviour.** All feelings are okay; not all actions are. "You're
  allowed to be furious; you're not allowed to hit." Never punish or dismiss the feeling itself.
- **Co-regulate, don't just correct.** Lend your calm in the storm; teach the coping later.
- **Model emotional health** — how you handle your own frustration, stress, and repair is the most
  powerful lesson.
- **Coach coping strategies** in calm moments — breathing, taking space, talking it through,
  problem-solving.
- **Build empathy** — talk about how others feel and why; point out others' emotions.
- **Avoid shame** — address actions, protect the child's sense of being fundamentally good.

**The one-line version.** Between six and ten children's emotions grow more complex (mixed feelings,
masked feelings, self-conscious emotions like pride and shame) and their ability to regulate them
slowly matures on a still-developing brain — so nurture it by naming all feelings, accepting
emotions while guiding actions, lending your calm, coaching coping skills, modelling emotional
health, and addressing behaviour without shaming the child.
