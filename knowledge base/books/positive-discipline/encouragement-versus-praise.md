# Encouragement vs praise — a subtle difference that matters

**They sound similar; they do opposite things over time.** Most of us were raised to praise
("good girl!", "well done!", "you're so clever!") and it feels loving. But Positive Discipline
draws a sharp, useful line between *praise* and *encouragement*. Praise rewards the finished
product and the child who pleases us; encouragement notices effort, progress, and the child's
own experience. Praise builds children who need applause to feel okay; encouragement builds
children who can feel okay from the inside.

**What praise quietly teaches.** Praise is a verdict handed down by a judge: "you (or your
work) meet my approval." Over time, a child raised mostly on praise can become:
- **Approval-dependent** — doing things for the reaction, deflating when no one claps,
  needing constant "was that good?"
- **Afraid of failure** — because praise is tied to success and to fixed traits ("you're so
  smart"), failing threatens the label, so they avoid risks that might cost it.
- **Externally steered** — looking to others to know their own worth.

**What encouragement builds instead.** Encouragement points at the process and hands the
judgment back to the child:
- **Effort and progress:** "You worked at that for a long time." "You've nearly got it — look
  how far you've come from last week."
- **The child's own feeling:** "You look really proud of that." "How do *you* feel about how
  it turned out?" This is the big one — it teaches self-evaluation rather than dependence on
  yours.
- **Contribution and belonging:** "Thank you, that really helped the family." "We couldn't
  have done it without you."
- **Faith and belonging regardless of outcome:** "I love being with you." "I have faith in
  you." — given whether they succeed or fail, so worth isn't performance-based.

**The tell: does it survive failure?** A simple test — praise usually only shows up when a
child *succeeds* ("great score!"). Encouragement works just as well when they *fail* ("that
was really hard and you kept going — I'm proud of how you stuck with it"). A child needs your
belief *most* in the moments they've fallen short, and that's exactly when praise goes silent
and encouragement shines.

**Encourage the discouraged child especially.** Remember that misbehaviour is discouragement
(see *the mistaken goals*). The child who has given up, who says "I can't," who acts out —
that child is starving for encouragement, not correction. Notice the smallest step, remove
criticism, and show unwavering faith. Encouragement is the single most powerful behaviour
tool you have, precisely because it feeds the belonging and capability that misbehaviour is
crying out for.

**Practical swaps:**
- "Good job!" → "You figured that out all by yourself."
- "You're so smart." → "You kept trying different ways until it worked."
- "Beautiful painting!" → "Tell me about your painting — what part do you like best?"
- "I'm so proud of you." (sometimes) → "You must be so proud of yourself." (builds inner pride)
- To a struggling child: "It's okay to make mistakes — that's how we learn. What could we try
  next?"

**Don't agonise over every word.** This isn't about banning "well done" or scripting yourself
into stiffness — a warm "well done!" now and then is fine. It's about *tilting* the balance,
over thousands of moments, from verdicts toward noticing effort, progress, feeling, and
contribution. Do that, and you raise a child whose sense of worth doesn't depend on the next
round of applause.

**The one-line version.** Praise rewards the pleasing result and breeds approval-dependence;
encouragement notices effort, progress, contribution, and the child's own pride — and it
still works when they fail, which is exactly when a child needs your belief the most.
