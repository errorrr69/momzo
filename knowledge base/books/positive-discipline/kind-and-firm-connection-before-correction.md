# Kind and firm at once — and connection before correction

**The both/and at the heart of it.** Most of us were raised to think discipline is a dial
between strict and soft — turn toward firm and you lose the warmth, turn toward kind and you
lose the control. Positive Discipline's core claim is that this is a false choice: the goal is
to be **kind and firm at the same time**, every time. Kind respects the child; firm respects
what needs to happen and the needs of the situation. Drop the kindness and you get
punishment; drop the firmness and you get permissiveness. Held together, they build children
who are both cooperative and secure.

**What "kind and firm" sounds like.** The trick is one sentence that honours the feeling
*and* holds the line:
- "I know you don't want to stop — *and* it's time to stop." (Not "because I said so," not
  giving in.)
- "I love you, *and* the answer is no."
- "You're allowed to be angry. I won't let you hurt your sister."
The word that stitches them is often *and*, not *but* — "but" erases the kindness that came
before it.

**Connection before correction — always in that order.** A child can only take in guidance
when they feel connected and safe; a child who feels attacked, shamed, or dismissed goes into
fight-or-flight and learns nothing. So before you correct, connect: get down to their level,
a warm hand, validate the feeling ("this is really disappointing"), maybe a bit of humour.
*Then* redirect or hold the limit. It feels counter-intuitive — surely misbehaviour should be
met with correction, not warmth? — but connection is what opens the door through which any
lesson can pass. "Children do better when they feel better," not when they feel worse. Trying
to teach a flooded, disconnected child is like posting a letter to a house with no letterbox.

**Act, don't talk.** Parents drown discipline in words — repeating, warning, explaining,
lecturing — and children quickly learn to tune the words out (and that you don't mean it until
the shouting starts). Positive Discipline says: fewer words, more calm action. Instead of
telling a child for the fifth time to get off the tablet, walk over, get their eye, and gently
help them hand it over. Instead of a lecture about shoes, hold the shoes out and wait warmly.
Kind, firm *action* teaches faster than a paragraph — and it keeps you out of the argument.

**Ask, don't tell — curiosity questions.** For older children especially (6–10 and up), a
"what and how" question teaches far more than a command or a lecture, because it makes the
child *think* rather than resist: "What's your plan for getting ready in time?" "What happens
to the paint if it stays on the floor?" "How could we solve this so it works for both of us?"
Genuine curiosity (not sarcastic "what did you THINK would happen?") invites the child to
find the answer themselves, which is where real learning and cooperation live.

**Decide what *you* will do, not what you'll make the child do.** A quietly powerful shift:
you can't actually control a child, and trying invites a power struggle you'll often lose. But
you can control *your own* actions, kindly and firmly. Rather than "you WILL eat at the
table," → "I'll serve dinner at the table; food stays there." Rather than forcing homework →
"I'm available to help until 7; after that I'm making dinner." You state what you'll do, follow
through calmly, and sidestep the battle entirely.

**Why the firmness must never slide into fear.** Firm means consistent and follow-through, not
harsh or scary. The moment firmness tips into intimidation, you've lost the kindness and
you're back to punishment, with all its costs. The image to hold is a calm, warm, unwavering
adult — not a soft one, and not a frightening one.

**The one-line version.** Be kind *and* firm at the same time — connect before you correct
(children learn only when they feel safe), lead with calm action and curiosity questions
instead of lectures, and decide what *you'll* do rather than trying to force the child, so
you hold the line without fear and without caving.
