# Self-regulation, impulse control, and big feelings

**Why impulsive, intense children act before they think.** Some children blurt out answers,
interrupt, grab, act on a whim, and swing into big emotions fast — then feel awful afterwards.
This isn't a lack of caring or a moral flaw; it's a slower-developing "brake." The brain's
impulse-control and emotional-regulation systems mature gradually, and in more active,
distractible children they often lag behind. The gap between *feeling* an urge or emotion and
*acting* on it is very short, so the behaviour is out before the thinking brain can intervene.
They usually know the rule perfectly well in a calm moment — they just can't reliably apply it in
the heat of one.

**Self-regulation is taught and practised, not demanded.** "Control yourself!" asks a child to
use a skill they're still building. Regulation grows through thousands of small experiences of
being calmed *by* a caring adult (co-regulation) and gradually learning to do it themselves. Your
calm is the first tool; explicit teaching in calm moments is the second.

**Co-regulation first: lend your calm.** When your child is flooded — furious, wound up,
melting down — they can't access reason. Meeting big feelings with your own big feelings pours
fuel on the fire. Instead, be the steady, calm presence they borrow: lower your voice, slow down,
stay near, name what they feel ("you're so frustrated that it didn't work"). Their nervous system
settles by syncing with yours. Only once they're calmer can any teaching happen.

**Teach the pause — the space between feeling and doing.** The core skill is inserting a gap
between impulse and action. Build it in calm moments and cue it gently in hot ones:
- A simple **"stop and think"** routine — practised as a game when things are calm so it's
  available later.
- **Name it to tame it** — helping a child put words to a feeling ("I'm really angry")
  activates the thinking brain and shrinks the feeling's grip. Children with a rich feelings
  vocabulary regulate far better, so narrate feelings often.
- **A calming toolkit** the child helps design — slow breaths (a long out-breath is the fastest
  off-switch for the body's alarm), counting, squeezing something, going to a cosy "calm corner"
  (as a self-soothing choice, never a punishment), or big physical output like running or jumping.
  Practise these *before* they're needed.

**Move the body to steady the mind.** For impulsive, high-energy children, physical activity is
one of the most reliable regulators there is. Regular movement, active play, and outdoor time
genuinely improve mood, focus, and self-control, and burning off energy *before* demanding
situations (before homework, before a shop trip) prevents a lot of blow-ups. Movement isn't the
enemy of self-control — it's one of its best supports.

**Set kind, firm limits on the behaviour while accepting the feeling.** Impulsivity is not an
excuse for hurting or breaking things; children still need clear boundaries. The trick is to hold
the limit while validating the feeling: "It's okay to be furious. It's not okay to hit. Let's get
that angry energy out safely." You're teaching that all feelings are allowed but not all actions —
and giving them a route for the feeling, not just a ban.

**Expect slip-ups and protect their dignity.** Progress is bumpy; there will be many moments the
brakes fail. Shaming an impulsive child ("what is WRONG with you?") deepens the belief that
they're bad, which drives worse behaviour. Respond to slips matter-of-factly, help them repair,
and keep noticing the moments they *do* pause or calm themselves — that's the muscle growing.
These children get corrected constantly; they need to hear far more about what they got right.

**When to seek extra support.** If impulsivity and emotional storms are frequent, intense, and
significantly affecting friendships, learning, or family life across settings, it's worth a calm
conversation with your child's doctor or school — not as a failure, but to get tools and support
tailored to your child.

**The one-line version.** Impulsive, intense children have a slower-developing brake, so
self-regulation must be taught, not demanded — lend your calm first (co-regulation), teach the
pause and a practised calming toolkit, use movement to steady them, hold kind-but-firm limits on
actions while accepting all feelings, and protect their dignity through the inevitable slip-ups.
