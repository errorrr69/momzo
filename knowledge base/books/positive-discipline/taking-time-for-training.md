# Take time for training — don't assume they know how

**The hidden assumption behind a lot of frustration.** A great deal of parental exasperation
comes from a quiet, wrong assumption: that a child *should already know how* to do the thing
we're asking — tidy a room, manage a morning, handle a disappointment, resolve a squabble — and
that when they don't, it's defiance or laziness. Positive Discipline flips this: most of the
time a child hasn't been *taught*. "Take time for training" means treating skills as things you
explicitly teach and practise, patiently, over time — not things a child is supposed to arrive
with.

**Why it works and where it saves you.** Every skill you take time to train is a future battle
you don't have to fight. Twenty minutes teaching a child *how* to clear their plate, pack their
bag, or calm their body pays back over months of smoother days. It also protects the
relationship: you stop being the frustrated nag ("why haven't you done it AGAIN?") and become
the coach who showed them how.

**How to train a skill (the steps):**
1. **Break it down.** What looks like one task ("tidy your room") is actually many steps to a
   child (books on the shelf, clothes in the basket, Lego in the box…). Name the steps.
2. **Show them — do it together first.** Model it, then do it *with* them, then let them do it
   while you watch, then let them do it alone. This gradual handover is how competence is built;
   jumping straight to "do it yourself" sets them up to fail.
3. **Keep expectations age-real.** A six-year-old's "tidy" and yours are different; a nine-
   year-old can handle more steps than a six-year-old. Match the task to the developmental stage
   and accept that early attempts will be imperfect.
4. **Let them practise — and let it be messy.** Skills need reps. Resist taking over when they're
   slow or clumsy; the fumbling *is* the learning. A job done imperfectly by the child beats a
   job done perfectly by you, every time, for what it builds.
5. **Encourage progress, not perfection.** Notice effort and improvement ("you got all the books
   away by yourself this time"), not just the finished standard.

**Train emotional and social skills, too — not just chores.** "Take time for training" isn't
only about practical tasks. Children also have to be *taught* how to calm down when angry, how
to ask for a turn instead of grabbing, how to disagree without hurting, how to lose a game
gracefully, how to say sorry and mean it. These don't arrive automatically at a certain age —
they're coached, in calm moments, through conversation, role-play, stories, and your own
modelling. A child who "can't control himself" often simply hasn't been shown *how* yet.

**Train in the calm, not the storm.** You can't teach a skill in the middle of a meltdown or a
fight — the learning brain is offline. The training happens *before and after*: practise the
calm-down routine when everyone's happy, so it's available when the storm hits; talk through
what to do differently *after* a conflict has settled. In-the-moment is for support; calm time
is for teaching.

**Redefine "misbehaviour" as a skill not yet learned.** When you catch yourself thinking "he
should know better," try swapping in "he hasn't reliably learned this yet — what can I teach or
practise?" This one reframe changes your whole stance from punishing a fault to coaching a
learner, which is both kinder and vastly more effective. Most "behaviour problems" at 6–10 are,
underneath, skill gaps waiting to be trained.

**The one-line version.** Don't assume children already know how — take time to explicitly teach
and practise skills (practical, emotional, and social), broken into steps, modelled and handed
over gradually, in calm moments not storms, so that most "misbehaviour" is met as a skill to be
coached rather than a fault to be punished.
