Building Blocks for Healthy Self Esteem

6-7 years·3 min

I'm rubbish at this!

The 30-second version

When your six-year-old collapses over a tiny mistake, they aren't just being dramatic. They are terrified of failing you. Instead of offering hollow praise or fixing the error for them, acknowledge the struggle and show them that messing up is a safe, normal part of learning.

Sound familiar?

Your seven-year-old scribbles violently over their homework page and throws the pencil.

You look and see a single slightly crooked letter on an otherwise beautiful page.

You are stuck between telling them it looks great—which they won't believe—and erasing it for them, which feels like agreeing it was a failure.

What's actually going on

  • At six and seven, they desperately want to feel capable and worry that making a mistake means they are no longer the good kid you are proud of.
  • Hollow praise like "it looks perfect" doesn't work because their own eyes tell them it isn't, which makes them feel misunderstood.
  • True confidence doesn't come from never slipping up, but from seeing that a slip-up doesn't change how much they are loved.

Skip these

  • Fixing the mistake for them
  • Insisting it looks perfect when they are unhappy with it
  • Telling them they are being dramatic

Try this instead

  • Acknowledge the frustration first: "It is so annoying when your hand doesn't do what your brain wants."
  • Focus on their effort rather than the end product: "Look how hard you worked on that line."
  • Give them control over the fix: "Do you want to leave it for now, or use the rubber and try again?"

Try tonight·The Oops Club

  1. At dinner, everyone shares one thing they messed up or found hard today.
  2. You start by sharing a real, small adult mistake: "I took the wrong turn and got us stuck in traffic."
  3. High-five each other for sharing, celebrating the courage it took to try.

Why it works: It normalizes mistakes, showing them that even the adults they look up to mess up and survive.

They are not fragile. They are just learning that mistakes are safe.

When your six or seven-year-old throws a pencil across the room because they drew a line slightly crooked, it feels like a massive overreaction. You might feel a sudden urge to fix it, to smooth things over, or to tell them it looks beautiful anyway. We want to protect them from that sharp, hot sting of frustration. But rushing in to rescue them actually sends a quiet, accidental message: You are right to be upset, because making a mistake is a disaster.

Real self-esteem isn't built on a foundation of constant praise or flawless performances. It is built when a child realizes they can survive a mistake and try again. At this age, school has introduced them to the concept of being evaluated. They are starting to compare themselves to their classmates, and they are incredibly sensitive to failure. They worry that a messy page, a dropped cup, or a missed ball means they are no longer capable, or that they have let you down.

When they cry "I can't do it," they are usually asking if they are still safe when they fail. Instead of arguing with their frustration by saying "yes you can," stay close to them. Let them know that learning is physically uncomfortable. You can say, "That is really tricky. Your fingers are still learning how to do what your brain is telling them." By naming the physical reality of the struggle, you take the shame out of it.

Finally, step back. Resist the urge to take the pencil and draw the line for them, or to take over the task. Give them small, real choices instead. Ask if they want to take a break, use an eraser, or try a different color. When they make the choice and eventually finish, they will feel a genuine sense of ownership. They will learn that confidence doesn't come from getting it right the first time, but from knowing they can handle getting it wrong.

When they want to quit the second it gets hard

The 30-second version

An eight-year-old who quits a game or homework at the first hurdle is trying to protect their ego from looking incapable. They want to be skilled immediately, and struggle when reality does not match their ideal self. Instead of pushing them to win, focus on their actual control: give them real choices, small responsibilities, and celebrate the messy middle.

Sound familiar?

Your nine-year-old slams their laptop shut during homework and yells, "I'm stupid, this math is impossible, and I am not doing it!"

You know they actually understand the concept, but they are terrified of getting a wrong answer and feeling foolish.

So you are stuck between two bad options: stepping in to do it for them just to end the tears, or forcing them to sit there, which only makes them feel more powerless.

What's actually going on

  • At this age, their self-esteem drops when they realize they are not naturally perfect at everything.
  • Quitting is a shield they use to reject the task before the task can reject them.
  • They need to feel in control of their progress rather than managed by your expectations.

Skip these

  • Taking over and finishing the task for them
  • Saying "but you are so smart" which actually increases the pressure to be perfect
  • Lecturing them about how giving up is for babies or will hurt their future

Try this instead

  • Acknowledge the friction without saving them: "It is incredibly annoying when your brain has to work this hard."
  • Offer a micro-choice to restore their agency: "Do you want to do the next two problems now, or take a five-minute break first?"
  • Praise the struggle, not the outcome: "I saw how long you stared at that puzzle before you figured it out."

Try tonight·The Draft Run

  1. When starting a hard task, grab a scrap piece of paper and label it "The Rough Copy."
  2. Tell your child that mistakes on this paper are actually required to train their brain.
  3. Let them scribble, scratch out, or make three wrong attempts before moving to the final version.

Why it works: It removes the pressure of instant perfection by making mistakes a planned part of the learning process.

They are not being lazy. They are protecting themselves from feeling incapable.

Around eight or nine, a shift happens. Your child is no longer just playing; they are comparing. When they sit down to do something and it does not immediately match that perfect picture, it hurts. To them, a mistake is not just a misstep; it feels like proof that they are fundamentally not enough.

When they shout that they quit and throw their pencil, they are trying to regain control. If they walk away first, they get to be the one who made the decision, rather than the kid who failed. It is a defense mechanism for a fragile, developing ego. As a mother, it is frustrating to watch them throw in the towel over something you know they can do. Your instinct might be to cheerlead them with praises, but to a child who feels incompetent in that moment, those words feel like a heavy weight they cannot lift.

To help them build real self-esteem, we have to lower the stakes of being wrong. This means shifting our focus from their natural talent to their effort and choice. When we step back and let them make decisions about how and when they tackle a challenge, we give them back their agency. They need to know that their position in the family and their value to you does not depend on them getting it right on the first try.

Instead of trying to fix the problem for them or shielding them from the struggle, let them feel the friction of learning. When they see that they can tolerate the frustration of a mistake and still come out okay on the other side, their confidence grows. They learn that competence is not something you are born with; it is something you build, one messy, imperfect attempt at a time.

is it tattling or telling

6-7 years·3 min

"Leo took the red Lego and it's my turn!"

The 30-second version

Six-year-olds tattle because they are obsessed with rules and want your approval, not because they are trying to be mean. When they run to you with minor complaints, they are checking if the rules still hold. Instead of getting annoyed, teach them the difference between keeping others safe and just reporting rule-breakers, then give them one simple line to say.

Sound familiar?

"Sam didn't put his shoes on the shelf and he's eating a biscuit!"

You know Sam is breaking a rule, but nobody is in danger and your child is looking at you, waiting for praise.

You are stuck between playing police officer for every tiny household rule, or telling them to stop snitching and making them feel dismissed.

What's actually going on

  • At six, rules are concrete boundaries that make their world feel safe and predictable.
  • They often report others to check if you still enforce the rules, seeking your reassurance and approval.
  • They genuinely cannot tell the difference yet between a dangerous situation and a harmless rule slip.

Skip these

  • Snapping at them to "stop tattling" or calling them a snitch.
  • Solving the minor problem for them immediately.
  • Ignoring them, which makes them feel like the rules do not actually matter.

Try this instead

  • Ask one simple question: "Is anyone hurt or unsafe?"
  • If everyone is safe, send them back with a direct phrase: "You can tell him, 'I am using this right now.'"
  • Praise them only when they report actual danger.

Try tonight·The Safe or Hurt Check

  1. Create a simple rule: we only report "hurt or danger" to grownups, and we handle "small things" ourselves.
  2. Practice with quick scenarios at dinner, like: "If someone takes your pencil, is that a tell or a tattle?"
  3. Give them the exact words for small things: "Please stop, I don't like that."

Why it works: It gives them a clear, logical filter to run through before they run to you.

They are not trying to be a snitch. They are checking if their world is still safe.

The full read — optional depth

At six and seven, your child is suddenly obsessed with rules. They are entering a wider social world of school and playgrounds where rules are the glue holding everything together. To a concrete thinker, a rule is absolute. If the rule is "we put our toys away," then anyone who leaves a Lego on the floor is disrupting the order of the universe. When they run to you to report this crime, they aren't trying to be malicious. They are trying to be your helper, checking to see if you are still policing the borders of their safe world.

This is why simply telling them "don't tattle" feels so confusing to them. To a young child, you are giving them highly contradictory information: you want them to follow the rules, but you get annoyed when they point out someone who isn't. They don't yet have the social maturity to understand nuance, nor do they have the deep empathy required to realize how annoying constant reporting can be to others.

To help them, we have to draw a clear line between what actually needs adult intervention and what doesn't. The simplest way to do this is to divide reports into "tattling" and "telling." Tattling is about trying to get someone in trouble or get attention for yourself. Telling is about protecting someone from getting hurt.

When they run to you with a complaint, take a beat. Ask them calmly, "Is anyone hurt or unsafe?" If the answer is no, hand the job back to them. Give them a direct, simple script they can use on the playground or with a sibling. This teaches them that they have the agency to handle minor friction, while keeping your door wide open for the big, scary things that actually require a grown-up's help.

"But I just want to watch one more video!"

The 30-second version

Using screens to soothe a tired six-year-old usually backfires into a bigger meltdown when it is time to turn it off. Instead of focusing only on strict hourly limits, look at what the device is replacing. Transition them with a physical, sensory task rather than a sudden countdown.

Sound familiar?

Your seven-year-old throws their school bag on the floor and shouts, "I need my tablet right now!"

You see the dark circles under their eyes and know they are completely spent from a long day of school.

You are stuck between giving in for twenty minutes of kitchen peace, or saying no and triggering an immediate, high-pitched floor tantrum.

What's actually going on

  • They are using the screen as an artificial off-switch to numb their exhausted brains after school.
  • Because digital media is designed to hold their attention, their brains cannot handle the sudden chemical crash when the screen disappears.
  • The meltdown is not bad behavior; it is a physical system overload from a body that forgot how to settle down naturally.

Skip these

  • Arguing about the clock or counting down minutes
  • Using the tablet as a reward for eating dinner
  • Snatching the device away mid-game

Try this instead

  • Acknowledge the exhaustion first: "Your brain is so tired from school today."
  • Offer a high-sensory, low-effort physical swap like a sink full of warm soapy water or some playdough.
  • Schedule a fixed, predictable screen window so they do not have to beg for it all afternoon.

Try tonight·The Water Transition

  1. As soon as they ask for a screen after school, fill the kitchen sink with warm water and bubbles.
  2. Drop in a few plastic cups or spoons and invite them to wash them.
  3. Stand nearby and let them splash quietly without asking them questions about their day.

Why it works: Warm water physically resets a highly stimulated nervous system, helping them wind down from school without the digital crash.

They are not being greedy for screens. They are trying to find an off-switch for their tired brains.

The full read — optional depth

When your child comes home from school, they are carrying a heavy load of invisible fatigue. Sitting still, following directions, and navigating playground politics for six hours takes an immense amount of physical and emotional control. By the time they cross your threshold, that control is entirely used up. When you announce that screen time is over, that artificial stimulation vanishes instantly. To break this cycle, we have to look at what the screen is crowding out. At six or seven, kids still need physical, sensory ways to discharge the tension of the school day. They need to move their muscles, feel different textures, and reconnect with you in a quiet space. If we transition them straight from the high-pressure classroom environment to a high-stimulation digital world, they never actually learn how to settle their own bodies.

Instead of fighting over minutes, try changing how they transition home. Give them a physical task that requires zero mental effort but offers rich sensory feedback. Let them jump on cushions, dig in a bin of dried beans, or wash plastic toys in a warm sink. By helping them land softly back in their physical bodies, you satisfy their need for calm and make it much easier for them to turn off the screen later without a fight.

When a toddler throws themselves on the floor because you cut their toast the wrong way, we expect it.
