1. Nurturing Independence
The "Small Helper" Cooking Station: Instead of doing the whole task yourself, break it into tiny steps your child can handle. Let them pour pre-measured milk into a bowl or collect eggshells in a small container to dump in the trash
.
The "Two-Choice" Wardrobe: To avoid a morning battle while building autonomy, offer exactly two or three outfits you’ve pre-selected. Praise their "great ability to make a choice" once they pick one
.
The Household Contribution Routine: Assign "mini-chores" that fit into daily flow, such as having a preschooler bring their own plate to the sink or helping load non-sharp items into the dishwasher
.
2. Managing Picky Eaters
Food Bingo: Create a game out of grocery shopping. Have your child pick out one new fruit or vegetable they think looks interesting and mark it off a "Bingo" card when they try it at home
.
DIY Meal Night: Set up a "Make-Your-Own" sub sandwich or pizza station. When kids are involved in the assembly and "stirring the bowl," they are statistically more likely to eat the result
.
The "Manchego" Exposure Scale: For older or more resistant eaters, have them rate new foods from 0 to 10. Any food they rate higher than a 5 gets added to the "practice list" to be served several times a week for a small prize
.
3. Giving Effective Instructions
The "Eye-to-Eye" Command: Instead of shouting from the kitchen, walk over to your child, get on their level, and give a direct statement like, "Please pick up the ball"
.
The Five-Second Processing Pause: After giving an instruction, stay silent and count to five in your head. This gives the child's brain time to process the request without the "noise" of repeated commands
.
The One-Step Challenge: For kids who get overwhelmed (especially those with ADHD), give only one instruction at a time—like "put on your sneakers"—rather than a long list of tasks
.
4. Tattling vs. Telling
The "Safe vs. Unsafe" Roleplay: Practice scenarios with your child using dolls or puppets. Ask them: "If someone touches your block, is that a Tattle (safe) or a Tell (unsafe)?"
.
The "Use Your Words" Script: Give your child a specific phrase for minor issues, like saying "It’s my turn" at a water fountain, instead of running to an adult immediately
.
5. Handling Transitions
The 5-Minute Warning Contract: Set a timer and make a "deal" before leaving a fun place: "When the timer goes off, we walk to the car. If you do it without a fuss, you get to pick the car music"
.
Visual Scheduling: For kids who crave predictability (like those on the autism spectrum), use a visual schedule to show that a "preferred activity" (like iPad time) isn't gone forever, but is just happening at a specific time later
.
6. Discouraging Lying
The 10-Minute "Truth Check": If you suspect a lie, say: "I’m going to walk away for 10 minutes. If you change your mind and want to give me a different answer when I come back, you won't get in trouble for the lie"
.
The "Preamble" Connection: Before asking a difficult question, remind your child: "I love you no matter what, and everyone makes mistakes. I just want an honest answer"
.
7. Positive Connection (P-R-I-D-E Skills)
Imitation Play: Spend 5-10 minutes a day following your child's lead in play. If they build a tower, you build a tower next to them. This "Imitation" acts as a powerful reward for good behavior
.
Labeled Praise: Instead of saying "Good job," use specific Description: "I love how you are sharing your crayons with your brother!"
.
8. Sleep Training Strategies
The Sleep Lady Shuffle: If you want a gentle approach, sit in a chair by the crib until they fall asleep, moving the chair slightly further toward the door every night until you are out of the room
.
The "Check-In" Interval: Put the child to bed and return to check on them at gradually increasing intervals (e.g., 5 mins, then 10 mins) to provide comfort without picking them up
.
9. Investigating the "Why" (Functional Behavior Analysis)
The ABC Diary: To stop a recurring bad behavior, keep a log for one week: What happened Antecedent (before), what was the Behavior, and what was the Consequence (your reaction)? This helps you see if they are acting out for attention, escape, or sensory needs
