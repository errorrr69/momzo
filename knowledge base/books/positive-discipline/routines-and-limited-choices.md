# Routines and limited choices — letting structure do the disciplining

**Two everyday tools that prevent more battles than any consequence.** A huge share of daily
conflict — mornings, mealtimes, bedtime, homework, screens — isn't really a discipline problem
at all; it's a *structure* problem. Positive Discipline offers two simple tools that quietly
remove the fights before they start: predictable **routines** and **limited choices**. Both
work by giving children the thing they crave (security and a sense of control) inside limits
you're happy with, so there's far less to rebel against.

**Routines: let the schedule be the boss, not you.** When the same things happen in the same
order every day, children stop fighting them, because they're no longer a personal demand from
you — they're just "how it goes." The routine becomes the authority. "It's not me nagging;
it's what the bedtime chart says comes next." This does something magic: it takes you out of
the role of enforcer and reduces the power struggle to almost nothing.
- **Build the routine *with* the child.** Especially for 6–10s, sit down together and map the
  steps (a morning or bedtime routine): what needs to happen, in what order. Children follow a
  plan they helped make far more willingly than one imposed on them.
- **Make it visual and let it lead.** A chart, list, or (for younger ones) photos of each step.
  Then, instead of directing, you point to it: "What's next on your chart?" The child checks
  the routine, not your face.
- **Consistency is the whole point.** Routines only work if they're reliable. The predictability
  itself is calming — children who know what's coming feel safe and cooperate more.

**Limited choices: control inside your boundary.** Much resistance is a child grasping for
some say over a life adults mostly run. You can satisfy that need without giving up the outcome
by offering two acceptable options:
- "Do you want to brush teeth before or after pyjamas?"
- "Do you want to walk to the car or hop like a frog to the car?"
- "Homework at the table or at the desk?"
Either answer gets you what you need; the child gets to steer. It sidesteps the "no" that a
flat command invites, because you've changed the question from *whether* to *which*.

**Rules for choices that actually work:**
- **Only offer choices you can live with.** Every option must be genuinely fine with you —
  don't offer "do you want to leave now?" if leaving now isn't optional.
- **Keep it to two (maybe three).** Too many options overwhelm a child and stall them.
- **The boundary is not up for choice — only the path to it is.** "Would you like to go to bed
  now or in five minutes?" keeps bedtime firm while handing over a scrap of control. "Do you
  want to go to bed?" is not a real choice and shouldn't be offered.
- **If they won't choose, you choose — kindly.** "Looks like it's hard to decide, so I'll pick
  this time, and you can choose next time." No drama.

**Why these beat nagging and rewards.** Nagging teaches children to wait for the nagging;
reward charts teach them to work for prizes. Routines and choices instead build genuine
capability and cooperation — the child internalises "this is how our mornings work" and "I get
a say in how I do things," which lasts long after any sticker chart would have stopped working.

**A note on the "witching hours."** The predictably hard times — the after-school slump, the
pre-dinner meltdown, bedtime — are exactly where routines pay off most. A reliable wind-down
sequence, a snack built into the schedule, a visual bedtime chart: these prevent the very
storms that no in-the-moment technique fully settles. Structure is prevention.

**The one-line version.** Prevent daily battles with predictable routines the child helps
build (so the schedule, not you, becomes the boss) and limited choices between options you're
happy with (so the child gets control inside your boundary) — structure disciplines more
gently and durably than any nag or reward.
