# Focus and the body — sleep, movement, and food

**Attention starts in the body, not just the brain.** Before reaching for focus techniques and
charts, it's worth knowing that a child's ability to concentrate, sit still, and manage
impulses rests heavily on three physical foundations: enough sleep, enough movement, and steady
fuel. When any of these is off, focus and self-control fall apart — and no strategy fully
compensates. For a child who already finds attention hard, getting the basics right often makes
the biggest single difference.

**Sleep — the quiet giant.** Under-slept children don't get sleepy and calm like tired adults;
they frequently get *more* wired, hyperactive, irritable, impulsive, and unfocused. Poor sleep
mimics and worsens exactly the attention and regulation struggles you're trying to help. School-
aged children (roughly 6–10) need a lot of sleep — commonly around 9–12 hours a night — and many
are quietly running short. Protect it:
- A consistent bedtime and a calm, predictable wind-down routine.
- **Screens off well before bed** — the light and stimulation delay sleep and wind children up.
- A cool, dark, quiet room; the same routine on weekends where possible.
If focus and behaviour are struggling, look hard at sleep first — it's often the cheapest,
highest-impact fix, and it's easy to underestimate how short a child is running.

**Movement — a genuine focus tool, not the enemy.** Physical activity is one of the most
reliable ways to improve attention, mood, and self-control, especially for active, distractible
children. Exercise helps the brain regulate itself; a child who has moved plenty focuses better
than one who's been still all day.
- Build in plenty of active play and outdoor time daily.
- Use movement *before* demanding tasks — a run around before homework primes the brain.
- Allow movement *during* focus (fidget, stand, wobble cushion, movement breaks) rather than
  forcing stillness that fights their wiring.
- Outdoor time and nature seem especially restorative for attention.

**Food — steady fuel, and skip the myths.** The brain runs on a steady supply of energy, so
*how* a child eats affects focus and mood more than any single "brain food":
- **Regular meals and snacks** keep blood sugar steady; a hungry child can't concentrate and is
  quicker to melt down. Never underestimate how much a well-timed snack (especially after school)
  prevents a "behaviour" problem.
- **Balanced meals** — including protein and slower-release carbohydrates rather than sugary
  quick hits alone — give more even energy than sugar spikes and crashes.
- **Breakfast matters** — starting the day fuelled supports morning focus.
- **Hydration** — even mild thirst dents concentration; make water easy to reach.
- **A myth worth dropping:** despite the popular belief, careful research has *not* shown that
  sugar itself causes hyperactivity in children generally. The bigger, real effect is steadiness
  — regular, balanced meals beat the roller-coaster of skipped meals and sugary snacks. (A small
  number of children are genuinely sensitive to specific foods; if you truly suspect a pattern,
  track it and talk to your doctor rather than guessing.)

**Screens deserve special mention.** Fast, intensely stimulating screen content can leave
attention-challenged children more restless, less able to tolerate slower real-world tasks, and
wound up before bed. This doesn't mean zero screens — it means being thoughtful about how much,
what kind, and especially *when* (not right before bed, not as the default downtime), and making
sure screens aren't crowding out sleep, movement, and unstructured play.

**Put the foundations first.** When a child's focus or behaviour is a struggle, run through the
basics before anything else: Are they sleeping enough? Moving enough? Fed and watered at regular
intervals? Getting outdoor and unstructured play? Fixing a shortfall in any of these often
improves things more than any clever technique — and makes every technique work better.

**The one-line version.** Focus and self-control rest on the body: prioritise plenty of sleep
(screens off before bed), daily movement (use it before and during focus, don't force stillness),
and steady fuel from regular balanced meals and water — get these foundations right before
reaching for any focus strategy, because nothing else fully makes up for them.
