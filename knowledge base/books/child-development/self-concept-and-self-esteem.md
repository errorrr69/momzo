# Self-concept and self-esteem (6–10)

**Middle childhood is when the self comes into focus.** As children move through the 6–10 years,
they develop a much clearer, more detailed, and more realistic picture of who they are. Younger
children describe themselves in concrete, often rosy terms ("I'm fast, I like dogs, I'm good at
everything"). School-age children build a more nuanced, psychological self-concept — describing their
personality, comparing themselves to others, and recognising they're better at some things than
others. This is also when self-esteem becomes differentiated and, crucially, increasingly based on
*how they measure up* to peers.

**Self-concept becomes more realistic and comparative.** Two shifts drive this:
- **Social comparison.** School-age children constantly compare themselves to others — who's faster,
  smarter, more popular, better at football. This is normal and even useful for a realistic
  self-view, but it means self-esteem becomes tied to where a child feels they rank, which can lift
  or wound them.
- **Domain-specific self-esteem.** Children stop having one global "I'm great/awful" and develop
  separate senses of competence in different areas — schoolwork, sport, looks, friendships,
  behaviour. A child can feel great about football and low about reading. Overall self-worth then
  depends heavily on how they feel about the areas *they* care about most, and on feeling
  fundamentally loved.

**What healthy self-esteem actually rests on.** Genuine self-esteem has two roots (see also the
gentle-discipline note on confidence):
1. **Feeling unconditionally loved and valued** — worthy just for being themselves, not for
   achievements or good behaviour. This is the deeper root, and it comes from warm relationships
   where love doesn't withdraw when the child fails or misbehaves.
2. **Feeling genuinely competent** — the earned sense of "I can do things," built by mastering real
   challenges through effort. Erikson called the central task of this age "industry vs inferiority":
   children need to develop a sense of competence and accomplishment, or they risk a lasting feeling
   of inferiority.

**Self-esteem powerfully shapes wellbeing and behaviour.** How a child feels about themselves affects
their willingness to try things, their resilience after failure, their friendships, their mental
health, and — importantly — their behaviour. Children with low self-worth often behave *worse* (a
child who feels bad about themselves acts out or gives up), while genuinely confident children take
on challenges and bounce back. Self-esteem isn't a "nice to have"; it's woven through everything.

**The traps that damage self-esteem.** Middle-childhood self-esteem is eroded by:
- **Conditional love** — warmth that depends on achievement or behaviour teaches worth is
  negotiable.
- **Comparison and labels** — being measured against siblings or peers, or given fixed labels ("the
  clever one," "my difficult child"), which children live up and down to.
- **Constant criticism** outweighing warmth — a stream of correction with little delight.
- **Empty, inflated praise** — "you're amazing at everything" rings hollow and doesn't build real
  competence; children see through it.
- **Overprotection** — rescuing children from every struggle whispers "I don't think you can,"
  starving them of the earned competence self-esteem needs.

**How parents build genuine self-esteem:**
- **Love unconditionally and show it** — make clear your love doesn't depend on success or good
  behaviour; separate the child from their actions ("I didn't like that choice; I love you the
  same").
- **Build real competence** — let children take on achievable challenges, struggle, and succeed;
  give real responsibility; resist over-rescuing.
- **Encourage effort and progress, not fixed traits** — praise the process ("you worked hard at
  that") over labels ("you're so smart"), which builds a growth mindset and resilience.
- **Help them find their strengths** — every child needs at least one area where they feel capable;
  help them discover and develop theirs.
- **Treat mistakes and failure as normal** — a child who fears failure has fragile self-esteem;
  make getting things wrong safe and useful.
- **Delight in who they are** — far more moments of being genuinely enjoyed than corrected; children
  build self-worth from feeling that their people light up around them.

**The one-line version.** In middle childhood children form a realistic, comparative self-concept and
domain-specific self-esteem shaped by how they measure up to peers — so build genuine self-worth on
its two roots: unconditional love (worthy for who they are) and earned competence (mastering real
challenges through effort), while avoiding conditional love, comparison, labels, empty praise, and
overprotection.
