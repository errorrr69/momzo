# The four "mistaken goals" behind misbehaviour

**A powerful lens: misbehaving children are discouraged children.** One of the most useful
ideas in Positive Discipline is that a child who is acting up is a child who has lost their
sense of belonging and significance — the two things every human most needs — and is
reaching for them in a mistaken way. The behaviour is a coded message: "I just want to
belong and matter, and I don't know how." When you can decode which mistaken goal is
driving the behaviour, you stop reacting to the surface and start meeting the real need. It
transforms discipline from a battle into detective work.

**How to spot which goal it is: check your own gut reaction.** The clever part of this
framework is that *your* feeling is the biggest clue to *their* goal. What you feel in the
moment, and what the child does when you correct them, tells you which of the four it is.

**1. Undue attention — "I belong only when you're noticing me."**
- *You feel:* irritated, annoyed, like you're constantly interrupted or nagged.
- *The child:* stops briefly when you attend to them, then starts again.
- *The coded message:* "Notice me. Involve me. Make me feel I count."
- *What helps:* give attention in positive ways *before* they have to grab it (fill the
  connection tank), give them a real job or role so they feel useful, and avoid the big
  reaction the behaviour is fishing for. Redirect to a useful task; catch them when they're
  *not* seeking attention.

**2. Misguided power — "I belong only when I'm the boss / when you can't make me."**
- *You feel:* angry, provoked, challenged, like "you can't get away with this."
- *The child:* digs in harder when pushed; may win, or stop only to defy again.
- *The coded message:* "Let me help. Give me a choice. Don't try to control me."
- *What helps:* step out of the power struggle entirely (it takes two — you can decline).
  Offer limited choices, invite their help, ask what needs doing rather than command, and
  give them appropriate control. You can be kind and firm without fighting.

**3. Revenge — "I don't feel I belong, so I'll hurt back like I've been hurt."**
- *You feel:* hurt, disappointed, disbelieving ("how could you?").
- *The child:* lashes out, says wounding things, breaks or retaliates; escalates if punished.
- *The coded message:* "I'm hurting. Help me with the hurt. I matter even now."
- *What helps:* don't retaliate or take the bait — that confirms their story. Stay warm,
  acknowledge the hurt underneath ("something's really hurting you"), avoid punishment, and
  rebuild the relationship. Revenge softens only when the child feels reconnected.

**4. Assumed inadequacy — "I don't believe I can belong, so I'll stop trying."**
- *You feel:* helpless, hopeless, like giving up on them too.
- *The child:* withdraws, won't try, gives up easily, "I can't."
- *The coded message:* "Don't give up on me. Show me a small step. Believe in me."
- *What helps:* break tasks into tiny wins, stop all criticism, notice any effort, and show
  faith relentlessly. This deeply discouraged child needs encouragement more than any other.

**Why this beats "what's the right consequence?"** Notice that the same behaviour — say,
refusing to get dressed — could be any of the four, and each needs a *different* response.
Reaching for a standard consequence without reading the goal often makes it worse (punishing
a revenge-seeking child fuels more revenge; over-helping an attention-seeker feeds it). The
goal tells you the fix.

**The through-line: encouragement, not punishment.** All four mistaken goals are forms of
discouragement, so the master antidote is always *encouragement* — helping the child feel
they belong and matter in real, healthy ways. A child who feels connected and capable has no
need for the mistaken route.

**A note for 6–10s.** These goals show up more subtly than in preschoolers — power looks like
sophisticated arguing; revenge like cutting words or coldness; inadequacy like "I'm rubbish
at this, why bother." The detective method still works: check your gut, decode the need,
meet it.

**The one-line version.** Misbehaviour is a discouraged child mistakenly chasing belonging
through undue attention, power, revenge, or giving up — read which one from your own gut
reaction, then meet the real need with encouragement instead of reacting to the surface.
