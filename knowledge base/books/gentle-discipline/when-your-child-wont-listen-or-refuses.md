# When your 6–10 year old won't listen or refuses

**Why this happens (it's rarely defiance for its own sake).** By six to ten, a child
can hear you perfectly well and still not move. Underneath a "no" there is almost
always a reason that makes sense to *them*: they're deep in something absorbing and
switching costs them effort, they're tired or hungry, they feel bossed around and are
protecting a scrap of control, or they simply don't yet have the brain wiring for
smooth transitions. The thinking, planning part of the brain is still years from
finished. A refusal is usually a signal about their state, not a verdict on your
authority.

**Why repeating and threatening backfires.** Saying the same instruction louder and
more times trains a child to wait for the escalation before responding — you
accidentally teach "she doesn't really mean it until the third time." Threats and
punishment can force compliance in the moment, but they work by fear, and fear crowds
out the very thing that makes a child *want* to cooperate: feeling connected to you and
safe with you. It also gives you nothing to escalate to next time except more fear.

**What to do instead — connect first, then direct.**
- **Get close and get their attention before you speak.** Come to their level, a hand on
  the shoulder, eye to eye. An instruction called across a room competes with everything
  else and usually loses.
- **Acknowledge what they're doing before you ask them to stop.** "You're right in the
  middle of your build — I can see it's a good one." A child who feels understood has far
  less reason to dig in.
- **Give a real heads-up.** Transitions are hard; a warning ("two more minutes, then
  shoes") lets the brain prepare instead of being yanked.
- **Offer control inside your boundary.** Two choices you're equally happy with —
  "shoes first or coat first?" — meet the need for autonomy without giving up the outcome.
- **Say it once, warmly, then follow through with your presence, not your volume.** Move
  over, stay calm, help them start. Younger-feeling days may need you to do the first step
  *with* them.

**Scripts you can borrow.**
- Instead of "How many times do I have to tell you?" → "It's hard to stop when you're
  having fun. It's time now — I'll walk over with you."
- Instead of "Because I said so." → "I know you don't want to. It still needs to happen.
  Do you want to do it the fast way or the silly way?"
- Instead of "If you don't come now, no screen time." → "Shoes are the last thing between
  us and the park. Race you to the door?"

**When "won't listen" is really something else.** Persistent, all-day refusal across many
settings can be a child telling you they're overwhelmed, anxious, not sleeping enough, or
struggling with something they can't name — sometimes at school. If the pattern is
constant rather than occasional, look at the load on the child before looking for a better
consequence. A sudden change is worth a gentle, curious conversation, not a crackdown.

**The repair matters more than the perfect response.** Every parent loses patience and
barks the third time. It doesn't undo your relationship. Coming back afterward — "I got
frustrated and raised my voice earlier; I'm sorry. Let's try that again" — teaches your
child more about handling hard feelings than a calm instruction ever could, and it keeps
the connection that makes cooperation possible.

**The one-line version.** A child who won't listen usually can't easily switch, feels
short of control, or needs you closer — so lower your voice, get near, name what they
feel, offer a small choice, and help them start. Connection first, direction second.
