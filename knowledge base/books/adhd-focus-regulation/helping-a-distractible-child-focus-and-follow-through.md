# Helping a distractible child focus and follow through

**The goal isn't to force focus — it's to remove the obstacles to it.** You can't will a
distractible child into concentrating, and nagging "concentrate!" just adds stress (which makes
focus worse). What works is changing the conditions around the child so that focusing gets
easier and following through becomes possible. Most of the wins here are practical and external,
not motivational.

**Cut the competition — set up the environment.** A distractible brain grabs whatever's most
interesting in the room, so reduce what it has to compete with:
- A clear, tidy workspace with only what's needed for this task; everything else out of sight.
- Away from windows, TV, busy areas, and — especially — screens and notifications.
- Some children focus better with quiet; others with low background noise or music without
  lyrics. Experiment and follow what works for *your* child.

**One instruction at a time — and make it stick.** Multi-step instructions overload a weak
working memory. Instead:
- Get their attention *first* — come close, eye contact, maybe a hand on the shoulder. An
  instruction thrown across a room is gone.
- Give one step, not a chain. "Put your shoes on." Then the next when it's done.
- Have them repeat it back, so you know it landed.
- Back up spoken words with visual reminders — a checklist, pictures, a note — so the child
  isn't relying on memory alone.

**Chunk tasks into small, visible pieces.** A big task ("do your homework," "tidy your room") is
overwhelming and stalls a child at the starting line. Break it into small steps, make them
visible (a checklist they tick off), and celebrate each one. "First just the ten spellings,
then a break" is doable where "do all your homework" is paralysing. Ticking off steps also gives
the little hits of progress that keep a distractible brain engaged.

**Work in short bursts with movement breaks.** Focus is a battery that drains faster for these
children. Short, timed work periods with real breaks in between beat long stretches — try a
burst of focused work followed by a few minutes of movement, and repeat. Physical movement in
the breaks (jumping, running to the garden and back, a quick dance) genuinely resets attention.
Let them fidget or stand while working if it helps; don't demand stillness that fights their
brain.

**Externalise memory and time.** Don't rely on the child to hold it all in their head — put it
in the world: checklists, visual schedules, sticky notes, a launch pad by the door for
tomorrow's things, alarms and timers for transitions. Make time *visible* with a timer or clock
they can see, and give countdowns before switches ("five minutes, then we pack up"). You're
lending them the skills their brain hasn't built yet.

**Use interest and novelty as fuel.** Since these brains run on interest, harness it: make
boring tasks a game or a race against a timer, add a silly element, connect the task to
something they love, and change things up when a system goes stale (what worked last month may
need a refresh). This isn't spoiling — it's working with how their attention actually engages.

**Catch them focusing — feedback that builds.** Distractible children get a torrent of negative
feedback ("stop, no, again, why haven't you…"). Deliberately notice and name the moments they
*do* focus, start, or finish: "You got straight into that — nice." Specific, genuine encouragement
of effort and follow-through builds both the behaviour and a self-image of "I can do this,"
which is fragile in these kids and worth protecting.

**Stay calm and patient — your regulation matters.** Follow-through will be inconsistent; there
will be forgotten steps and abandoned tasks. Meeting that with frustration adds stress and shuts
the child down further. Steady, matter-of-fact re-direction ("looks like you got sidetracked —
what's next on your list?") keeps them able to re-engage without shame.

**The one-line version.** You can't force a distractible child to focus, but you can make
focusing possible: clear the environment, give one step at a time with visual back-up, chunk
tasks into tick-off pieces, work in short bursts with movement breaks, put memory and time out in
the world, use interest as fuel, and notice every success — all with calm patience.
