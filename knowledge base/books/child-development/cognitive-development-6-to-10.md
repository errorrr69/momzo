# Cognitive development in middle childhood (6–10)

**A genuinely new kind of thinking arrives.** The years from about six to ten bring one of
childhood's biggest mental shifts. In Piaget's terms, children move into "concrete operational"
thinking: they become logical, organised reasoners about real, concrete things. The magical,
easily-fooled thinking of the preschool years gives way to a child who can reason, classify,
order, and understand cause and effect — as long as it's grounded in the tangible and real. Abstract
and hypothetical reasoning ("what if the world had no gravity?") mostly waits for adolescence.

**What school-age children can newly do:**
- **Conservation** — they understand that quantity stays the same despite changes in appearance
  (the same water poured into a tall thin glass isn't "more"). Younger children are fooled by how
  things look; school-age children reason about the underlying reality.
- **Logical classification and ordering** — they can sort things into categories and hierarchies
  and arrange them in series (by size, number, time). This underpins maths and organised thinking.
- **Reversibility** — they can mentally reverse a process (if 4+3=7, then 7−3=4), a foundation for
  arithmetic.
- **Less egocentric thinking** — they increasingly grasp that others see, know, and feel
  differently from themselves, which transforms both problem-solving and friendship.
- **Cause and effect** — they reason more systematically about why things happen.

**Attention gets stronger and more controllable.** Compared with younger children, 6–10 year olds
can concentrate for longer, focus on what's relevant while ignoring distractions, and deliberately
direct their attention. This is still developing (and varies a lot between children — see the notes
on focus and executive function), but the growth is real and is a big part of why formal schooling
starts around this age.

**Memory becomes strategic.** Younger children remember somewhat haphazardly; school-age children
start using deliberate memory *strategies* — rehearsing (repeating to remember), organising
information into groups, and linking new facts to what they already know. They also develop
**metacognition**: thinking about their own thinking. They begin to know what they know, notice when
they don't understand, and (with support) plan and check their own learning. These skills can be
explicitly taught, which is a gift for helping children learn.

**Language and knowledge expand hugely.** Vocabulary grows fast, grammar becomes sophisticated, and
children this age start to appreciate that words have multiple meanings — enabling jokes, riddles,
sarcasm, and wordplay (the "knock-knock joke" age). They can follow more complex explanations, hold
a real conversation, and use language to reason and plan. Their general knowledge about the world
balloons, and each new chunk of knowledge makes learning the next thing easier.

**Thinking is still concrete — respect that.** The crucial caveat for parents and teachers: 6–10
year olds reason well about *concrete, real, present* things but struggle with the purely abstract.
Moral rules, time, money, and ideas land best when made tangible and connected to real experience.
Long abstract lectures ("you need to be more responsible") often sail past; concrete examples,
hands-on demonstration, and stories land. Meeting children at concrete-operational level — showing,
not just telling — is how you actually reach them.

**Huge individual variation is normal.** Children of the same age can be at quite different points —
in attention, memory, reasoning, and knowledge — all within the normal range, shaped by
temperament, experience, and opportunity. Development is also uneven *within* a child: a nine-year-
old may reason like an older child about dinosaurs (a passion) and a younger one about tidying.
Comparison to other children is rarely useful; the child's own progress is what matters.

**How to support cognitive growth (mostly through everyday life):**
- **Talk richly and explain** — conversation, questions, and "why" discussions build thinking and
  language.
- **Make abstract things concrete** — use objects, drawings, real examples, and hands-on activities.
- **Teach in the "zone"** — pitch challenges just beyond what they can do alone, and scaffold.
- **Encourage curiosity and problem-solving** — let them puzzle things out rather than handing
  answers; ask "what do you think?"
- **Read together and play** — both are powerful engines of cognitive and language development.
- **Support memory and metacognition** — teach simple strategies (grouping, linking, checking) and
  praise the thinking process.

**The one-line version.** Between six and ten children become logical, organised thinkers about
concrete, real things — grasping conservation, classification, and cause-and-effect, attending
longer, using memory strategies and thinking about their own thinking, and exploding in language and
knowledge — while still needing the abstract made concrete, so support them with rich talk, hands-on
examples, curiosity, reading, and challenges pitched just beyond their reach.
