# The major theories of child development

**Why theories are useful to a parent.** Theories are just organised ways of understanding how
children grow — different lenses, each capturing something real. No single one explains everything;
together they give a rich picture. Knowing the main ones helps a parent make sense of their child
and cut through conflicting advice, because most parenting ideas trace back to one of these.

**Attachment theory (Bowlby, Ainsworth).** Children are biologically driven to form close bonds
with their caregivers, and the quality of that early bond shapes their sense of safety and their
later relationships. A child who experiences warm, consistent, responsive care tends to develop a
*secure attachment* — using the parent as a "safe base" to explore from and a "safe haven" to
return to — which predicts better emotional regulation, confidence, and relationships. This is the
theory behind "connection first": a securely attached child cooperates and thrives because they
feel safe and loved. Attachment keeps mattering well beyond infancy.

**Piaget's cognitive stages.** Children think in *qualitatively different ways* at different ages,
actively constructing their understanding by exploring the world — not just knowing less than
adults, but reasoning differently. His broad stages: sensorimotor (infancy, learning through senses
and action), preoperational (roughly 2–7, imaginative but not yet logical, struggling to see
others' viewpoints), concrete operational (roughly 7–11 — the 6–10 zone — logical about concrete,
real things, grasping conservation and cause-and-effect), and formal operational (adolescence,
abstract and hypothetical thinking). The key takeaway: meet children at their actual stage; a young
child literally can't yet think the way an older one can.

**Vygotsky's social learning.** Children learn largely *through* social interaction and their
culture, not alone. His famous idea, the "zone of proximal development," is the gap between what a
child can do alone and what they can do with help from someone more skilled. Learning happens best
in that zone, through "scaffolding" — a supportive adult or peer providing just enough help, then
gradually withdrawing it as the child masters the skill. This is the theory behind teaching by
doing-it-together-then-fading, and behind the power of rich talk and guided play.

**Erikson's psychosocial stages.** Development is lifelong, and at each stage a person works
through a central emotional challenge. For 6–10 year olds, the key one is **"industry vs
inferiority"**: children this age are driven to master skills, make things, and feel competent. When
they experience success and encouragement, they develop a sense of industry and capability; when
they meet constant failure or criticism, they can develop a lasting sense of inferiority. This is
why encouragement, achievable challenges, and celebrating effort matter so much at this age — you're
building a child's belief that they're capable.

**Behaviourism and social learning (Skinner, Bandura).** Behaviour is shaped by its consequences
(what's rewarded tends to repeat, what's ignored tends to fade) and, crucially, by *observation* —
children learn powerfully by watching and imitating the people around them (Bandura). This is the
science behind "model what you want to see": children copy how you handle anger, honesty, and
kindness far more than they follow what you say. It also cautions that heavy reliance on rewards and
punishments has limits (they shape surface behaviour, not inner motivation).

**Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems.** A child develops inside nested layers of environment: the
immediate settings they're in (family, school — the microsystem), the connections between those,
the wider community and parents' workplaces, and the overarching culture and society — all
interacting, and all changing over time. The practical lesson: a child is shaped by far more than
their parents, and supporting a child means paying attention to the whole ecosystem around them.

**Information-processing.** This view compares the mind to a computer, focusing on *how* children
take in, store, and use information — attention, memory, and thinking strategies — and how these
grow more efficient with age. It's especially useful for understanding school-age learning:
older children attend better, remember more, and use smarter strategies than younger ones, and can
be taught those strategies.

**How to hold all these at once.** Don't pick one — use them together. Attachment explains the need
for connection; Piaget and information-processing explain how thinking grows; Vygotsky explains how
to teach; Erikson explains the emotional task of the age; behaviourism/social learning explains the
power of modelling and consequences; Bronfenbrenner reminds you of context. A parent who
understands a bit of each has a genuinely rich, balanced map of their child.

**The one-line version.** The major theories are complementary lenses — attachment (the need for a
secure bond), Piaget (thinking develops in stages), Vygotsky (children learn through guided social
interaction), Erikson (6–10s are driven to feel competent), behaviourism/social learning (children
copy models and respond to consequences), Bronfenbrenner (nested contexts shape the child), and
information-processing (attention and memory grow with age) — and together they give parents a full,
balanced understanding of how children grow.
