# Handling aggression — hitting, kicking, throwing, breaking things

**First principle: aggression is a stress behaviour, not a character flaw.** When a child
hits, kicks, bites, throws, or breaks something, their emotional brain has flooded and the
thinking brain that would normally hold the impulse has gone offline (see *why children
misbehave and the brain*). The child is not "being violent"; they are overwhelmed and have
run out of better tools in that instant. This is true even at eight or nine — older children
have bigger bodies and can do more damage, but the underlying mechanism is the same loss of
control under a surge of feeling.

**Your first job is safety, calmly.** In the moment, you are not teaching — you're
containing. 
- **Stop the harm with your body, not your volume.** Move between the children, gently
  block the throwing arm, take the object, get down to their level. "I won't let you hit.
  I'm going to keep everyone safe." Firm, kind, physical, few words.
- **Stay as calm as you can fake.** Meeting rage with rage pours fuel on it; your steadiness
  is what their nervous system borrows to come down. This is the hardest part and the most
  important.
- **Get them to a calmer place if needed** — not as banishment ("go to your room!") but as
  help ("let's get out of here and cool down"). Ideally stay with them; a flooded child
  often needs a calm adult *nearby*, not to be sent away to manage alone.

**Only teach once the storm has passed.** A child mid-meltdown cannot absorb a lesson.
Later — minutes or hours on, when everyone's regulated — you do the real work:
- **Name what happened without shame.** "You got so angry that your body hit. That happens
  when feelings get too big."
- **Make the boundary clear.** "Hitting hurts, and it's my job to keep everyone safe, so I
  won't let it happen. We'll find another way for the anger to come out."
- **Teach the replacement.** Anger isn't the problem — what we *do* with it is. Give real
  alternatives and practise them in the calm: stamp feet, punch a cushion, squeeze a
  toy hard, run outside, roar, come find you and say "I'm SO angry." A child needs a
  *route* for the feeling, not just a ban on the old one.
- **Repair.** Help them put things right in a way that isn't a forced, hollow "sorry":
  check on the sibling, fetch a cold cloth, help rebuild what they knocked down. Repair
  teaches responsibility far better than punishment.

**Hunt for the triggers — aggression is usually predictable.** Track when it happens and a
pattern almost always appears: the after-school "witching hour" when they're depleted,
hunger, tiredness, over-stimulation, coming down from screens, a sibling in their space, or
transitions. Prevention beats intervention every time — a snack and a wind-down at 4pm can
erase a "behaviour problem" that no consequence ever touched.

**Destructive behaviour (breaking, throwing, wrecking) reads the same way.** Sometimes it's
flooded rage; sometimes it's a child seeking big sensory input or testing cause-and-effect;
sometimes it's the only "loud" way they know to show how bad they feel. Respond the same:
stop it safely, stay calm, get curious about the need, teach a safe outlet for big physical
energy (jumping, heavy play, tearing newspaper, outdoor time), and have them help repair
the damage.

**What to avoid:**
- **Hitting back or "smacking to show it hurts."** It teaches that hurting is how the
  powerful get their way — the exact belief driving the aggression — and damages trust.
- **Big punishments after the fact.** They add shame without teaching the missing skill, and
  a child who feels bad about themselves tends to behave *worse*, not better.
- **Shaming labels.** "You're a bully / so aggressive / a bad boy" become a story the child
  lives up to. Separate the child from the behaviour: "you're a kind kid who had a really
  hard, angry moment."

**When to reach for more help.** Occasional aggression under stress is normal childhood.
But if it's frequent, intense, aimed to seriously hurt, comes with other worries, or you
feel out of your depth, that's not a discipline failure — it's a signal to loop in your
child's doctor or a child mental-health professional. Persistent aggression is often a
child asking for support they can't name.

**The one-line version.** Aggression is an overwhelmed brain out of tools — keep everyone
safe with calm firmness in the moment, teach a safe route for the anger and repair once
they're regulated, prevent the predictable triggers, and never answer hurting with hurting.
