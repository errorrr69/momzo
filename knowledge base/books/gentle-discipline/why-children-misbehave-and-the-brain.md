# Why children misbehave — and what their brain has to do with it

**Start here: "misbehaviour" is almost always communication.** When a six-to-ten year old
acts out, it looks like a choice to be difficult. Underneath, it is nearly always a child
whose needs have outrun their ability to cope: they are tired, hungry, overwhelmed,
anxious, over-stimulated, disconnected from you, or being asked for a skill they don't yet
reliably have. Behaviour is the visible tip; the need is the part under the water. A parent
who asks "what is my child telling me they can't yet manage?" solves the real problem far
faster than one who asks "how do I make this stop?"

**The brain fact that changes everything: the "upstairs" brain is a construction site.**
Children have a fast, powerful emotional brain (the part that feels fear, anger,
excitement, urgency) from very early on. The part that plans, waits, sees another view,
weighs consequences, and puts feelings into words — the prefrontal cortex, the "upstairs"
brain — is still years from finished and keeps developing into the mid-twenties. So a
6–10 year old *feels* like an adult in intensity but *steers* like a learner driver. When
a strong feeling floods in, the upstairs brain can go briefly offline. In that moment your
child is not choosing to be unreasonable; they have temporarily lost access to the very
skills you're asking them to use.

**What "flipping their lid" looks like at 6–10.** A younger child melts down on the floor.
An older one slams a door, shouts "I hate you," refuses to move, or goes cold and silent.
Same mechanism: the emotional brain has taken the wheel. You cannot reason, teach, or
negotiate with a child in that state — the part that would receive the lesson isn't
available. First you help them feel calm and safe; *then*, once the upstairs brain is back
online, there's a child there who can actually learn.

**Common real reasons behind everyday "bad" behaviour:**
- **A skill gap, not a will gap.** Waiting, sharing attention, switching tasks, handling
  disappointment, and staying organised are all skills that develop unevenly. A child who
  "should know better" may know it in calm moments and lose it under load.
- **Disconnection.** Children need to feel securely connected to you. When that tank runs
  low — you've been busy, distracted, on your phone, or there's tension at home — they
  often top it up with "negative" attention, because any attention beats none.
- **A bid for control.** Children have very little say over their day. Digging in over
  shoes, food, or bedtime is often less about the shoes and more about needing *some*
  power somewhere.
- **Unmet physical needs.** Tiredness, hunger, thirst, too much screen time, too little
  movement, and coming down from excitement all shorten the fuse dramatically.
- **Big feelings with nowhere to go.** Worry about school, a friendship, or a change at
  home frequently leaks out sideways as irritability or defiance at home — home is where
  it's safe enough to fall apart.
- **Development doing its job.** Testing limits, arguing, and pushing back are not faults;
  they are how children learn where the edges are and build a self that is separate from
  you.

**Why "he did it on purpose" is usually the wrong lens.** Older children *can* be
deliberate, and it can feel personal. But labelling behaviour as manipulation or
attention-seeking tells you nothing you can act on and quietly casts your child as an
opponent. Reframing it as "a need I haven't spotted yet" keeps you on the same team and
points you at a fix. Even genuinely attention-seeking behaviour is just a child asking, in
the only way that's working, for connection they're short of.

**What this means you should actually do:**
- **Regulate yourself first.** A calm adult brain is the fastest way to bring a child's
  brain back online — they borrow your steadiness (this is co-regulation). If you're
  flooded too, nothing good will happen next; steady yourself before you respond.
- **Connect before you correct.** Get close, get calm, name the feeling ("you're really
  angry the game got turned off"). Feeling understood is what settles the emotional brain.
- **Teach the missing skill in the calm, not the storm.** The moment of meltdown is for
  safety and soothing, not lessons. Later, when everyone's regulated, you can problem-solve
  and practise the skill that was missing.
- **Look for the pattern.** Repeated flashpoints at the same time of day almost always
  point to a predictable need — hunger, tiredness, the transition home from school, the
  screen-off moment. Fix the pattern and the "behaviour" often dissolves.

**The one-line version.** Children misbehave because a need has outrun a still-developing
brain — so read the need under the behaviour, lend your calm, connect before you correct,
and teach the missing skill once the storm has passed.
